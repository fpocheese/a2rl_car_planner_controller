\author[bit]{Shuyi Gao}
\author[bit]{Ren Jin}
\author[bit]{Defu Lin}

\begin{abstract}
Interactive overtaking requires more than choosing a passing side: the tactical
decision must persist as the opponent responds and must expose enough space for
a downstream planner to execute it. Direct game solvers capture the interaction
but are costly online; discrete maneuver labels and single trajectories do not
provide a persistent planner contract; and end-to-end policies hide whether a
decision is executable. This paper introduces a game-guided tactical-corridor
policy that makes a time-varying feasible set, rather than an actuator command,
the learned decision variable. A Stackelberg game with iterative best responses
supplies an action prior and a robust-value target to Truncated Quantile Critics,
amortizing repeated interaction reasoning into one policy evaluation. The
policy outputs a passing-side commitment, speed cap, and Frenet corridor; a
hysteretic state machine and deterministic carver preserve the selected side
and reject collapsed intervals. Candidate actions are projected through the
identified tire-force feasibility model, and a common corridor-constrained
path--speed planner and model predictive contouring controller produce
throttle, brake, steering, and gear commands. The method was evaluated on a
hardware-in-the-loop testbed in which
the complete algorithm ran on the target Neousys RGS-8805GC computer and
communicated through two Controller Area Network (CAN) channels with an EAV24
simulator on a
separate server. The plant configuration includes subsystem-level powertrain,
brake, steering, suspension, aerodynamic, thermal-tire, and sensor dynamics.
Across 1000 closed-loop evaluation laps, the full method achieved a 94.7\%
overtaking success rate (95\% exact binomial confidence interval,
93.1--96.0\%) with no observed collision or track-boundary violation. Matched
comparisons and component ablations attribute the gain primarily to learned
corridor shaping, game-derived guidance, and persistent side commitment. The
evidence is limited to two-vehicle engagements with simulator-provided state
and opponent information.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
% =====================================================================
Autonomous racing exposes a control system to a regime in which tactical
interaction and tire-force limits evolve on comparable time scales. A
trailing vehicle must select an overtake before the available space closes,
yet that decision is useful only if the downstream planner can construct a
continuous path and the controller can realize it through the physical
actuator interface. Full-scale events such as the Abu Dhabi Autonomous Racing
League (A2RL) make this coupling practically consequential because racing
software runs on vehicle hardware, observes other non-cooperative agents, and
must return drive-by-wire commands under a hard real-time loop
~\cite{betz2022survey,hoffmann2026a2rl}. The central control problem is therefore
not merely collision avoidance or low-level tracking. It is how to turn
opponent-aware tactical reasoning into a passing opportunity that remains
geometrically explicit, temporally persistent, and executable under repeated
replanning.

\subsection{Related work}

Existing racing systems represent tactical intent in three main ways.
Rule-based state machines and maneuver libraries are transparent and have
been deployed successfully, but their fixed thresholds require extensive
track-specific tuning~\cite{betz2019architecture,kabzan2020amzracing,
jung2023iac}. Dynamic-game formulations couple the competitors' plans through
best responses or equilibrium concepts, including Stackelberg and potential
games~\cite{liniger2019game,wang2021game,fridovich2020ilqgames,
laine2021multi}. Their strategic semantics
are attractive, although repeated equilibrium computation and sensitivity to
opponent models complicate execution at racing rates. Reinforcement learning
can instead amortize repeated interaction and has achieved strong racing
performance~\cite{wurman2022gtsophy,fuchs2021superhuman,
song2021overtaking,lin2023limit}. Recent Bayesian and hybrid formulations add
model uncertainty or learned strategic guidance~\cite{jia2023bayesian,
kalaria2025alpha}. End-to-end policies, however, generally hide
the geometric feasibility assumptions needed by a model-based planner and
controller.

Hierarchical approaches partially address this gap by separating tactical
choice from trajectory generation~\cite{thakkar2024hierarchical,
hoffmann2026a2rl}. In many implementations, the interface remains a discrete
mode, a target point, or a single predicted trajectory. These representations
do not state how much track space is reserved, when a passing side becomes
committed, or whether the remaining region stays useful under the next
receding-horizon update. Treating the opponent only as a predicted moving
obstacle can therefore produce a locally feasible but strategically frozen
plan. Conversely, directly learning steering and longitudinal commands removes
the opportunity for deterministic geometric and actuator checks. These
alternatives leave three linked gaps: repeated game solutions are too expensive
for the tactical rate, reward-only learning has weak structural guidance for
rare passing windows, and transient tactical outputs can switch sides or
collapse before the planner finishes the maneuver. A time-varying Frenet
corridor provides the missing interface. It exposes the chosen passing
opportunity as explicit inequalities while leaving path regularity, dynamic
feasibility, and actuator tracking to established optimization modules.

\subsection{Contributions and paper organization}

This paper develops a game-guided tactical corridor policy for interactive
overtaking. A Stackelberg game with iterative best responses (IBR) provides
the strategic decomposition, a prior action, and an auxiliary robust-value
target. A Truncated Quantile Critics (TQC) policy amortizes this computation
and selects continuous corridor parameters together with a persistent passing
side. A deterministic state machine and corridor carver then convert the
policy output into auditable bounds for a common execution stack.
The downstream stack is deliberately held fixed in all comparisons. It serves
as the execution layer needed to test the tactical contribution rather than
as a separate claim of novelty.

The contributions are threefold.
\begin{enumerate}
    \item We formulate tactical overtaking as interaction-aware feasible-set
    selection and derive a game-guided TQC objective that transfers both an
    IBR-consistent action prior and a robust game-value target. This construction
    amortizes repeated Stackelberg reasoning while limiting optimistic value
    estimates for rare, high-return passing sequences.

    \item We introduce a planner-compatible hybrid interface that combines
    a learned time-varying corridor with hysteretic side commitment,
    deterministic opponent-footprint carving, and physical-feasibility
    projection. It prevents update-to-update side switching and corridor
    collapse while publishing lateral bounds and a speed cap that can be
    inspected independently of the learned policy.

    \item We evaluate the tactical method in a target-hardware HIL loop. The
    complete stack runs on the A2RL RGS-8805GC computer and exchanges vehicle,
    opponent, race-control, and four-channel actuator data with an EAV24 plant
    through two Controller Area Network (CAN) channels. Matched baselines and
    ablations test tactical effectiveness,
    component necessity, and closed-loop failure modes.
\end{enumerate}

Section~\ref{sec:model} defines the decision variables and common feasibility
models. Section~\ref{sec:tactical} presents the game-guided corridor policy.
Section~\ref{sec:execution} summarizes the common corridor-constrained
execution layer. Section~\ref{sec:experiments} reports the HIL evaluation and
Section~\ref{sec:discussion} discusses the evidence and its limitations.

\section{Problem Formulation and Feasibility Models}\label{sec:model}
% =====================================================================
Consider an ego vehicle and a non-cooperative opponent on a closed track. At
each tactical update, the ego receives their measured states, local track
geometry, and race-control status. It must publish a mode, a committed passing
side, a speed cap, and lateral bounds over a finite spatial horizon. The output
is admissible only if the resulting corridor lies within the track, excludes
the opponent footprint with the prescribed clearance, and contains a
path--speed reference compatible with the identified tire-force envelope. This
section defines the common vehicle, Frenet, and acceleration-limit models used
to test those conditions. These reduced-order models belong to the algorithm;
the HIL plant described in Section~\ref{subsec:hil_env} is independently
configured and is not integrated from these equations.

Let the continuous-time controller state and input be
\begin{equation}
\bm{x}=\bigl[X,\;Y,\;\psi,\;v_x,\;v_y,\;r,\;\delta,\;s_p\bigr]^\top,\qquad
\bm{u}=\bigl[a,\;\dot\delta\bigr]^\top ,
\label{eq:state}
\end{equation}
where $(X,Y)$ is the inertial position, $\psi$ is yaw angle, $(v_x,v_y)$ are
body-frame longitudinal and lateral velocities, $r$ is yaw rate, $\delta$ is
front steering angle, and $s_p$ is an auxiliary progress state used by
the simulation framework for lap counting.  The input $a$ is the commanded longitudinal
acceleration and $\dot\delta$ is the steering-rate command.  The rigid-body
kinematics are
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\dot X&=v_x\cos\psi-v_y\sin\psi,&
\dot Y&=v_x\sin\psi+v_y\cos\psi,\\
\dot\psi&=r,& \dot s_p&=v_x .
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:kin}
\end{equation}
The front and rear slip angles are
\begin{align}
\alpha_f &= \arctan\!\left(\frac{v_y+l_f r}{v_x+\varepsilon_v}\right)-\delta,
\label{eq:alphaf}\\
\alpha_r &= \arctan\!\left(\frac{v_y-l_r r}{v_x+\varepsilon_v}\right),
\label{eq:alphar}
\end{align}
where the small positive $\varepsilon_v$ regularizes the low-speed
denominator.  With linear lateral tire stiffness,
\begin{equation}
F_{yf}=-C_f\alpha_f,\qquad F_{yr}=-C_r\alpha_r .
\label{eq:tire}
\end{equation}
The resulting Newton--Euler equations are
\begin{align}
\dot v_x &= a+r v_y-\frac{1}{m}F_{yf}\sin\delta,
\label{eq:vxdot}\\
\dot v_y &= \frac{1}{m}\bigl(F_{yf}\cos\delta+F_{yr}\bigr)-r v_x,
\label{eq:vydot}\\
\dot r &= \frac{1}{I_z}\bigl(l_fF_{yf}\cos\delta-l_rF_{yr}\bigr).
\label{eq:rdot}
\end{align}
The steering angle evolves as $\dot\delta=u_\delta$ where $u_\delta$ is the
steering-rate input in~(\ref{eq:state}).
Equations~(\ref{eq:kin})--(\ref{eq:rdot}) define
\begin{equation}
\dot{\bm{x}}=f_c(\bm{x},\bm{u}),\qquad
\bm{x}_{k+1}=f_d(\bm{x}_k,\bm{u}_k),
\label{eq:fd}
\end{equation}
where $f_d$ is the fourth-order Runge--Kutta discretization with sampling
time $T_s$.  Numerical parameter values are collected in
Table~\ref{tab:setup_params} in \ref{app:parameters}.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{model_shuoming.pdf}
\caption{Vehicle and track model in the Frenet frame.}
\label{fig:vehicle_track_model}
\end{figure}

The linear lateral-force relation in Eq.~\eqref{eq:tire} is the local
small-slip approximation used by the reduced-order controller model.  The
load-dependent nonlinear characteristics used to construct the physical
feasibility limits are represented by the pure-slip Magic Formula
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\zeta_j&=B_jq_j-E_j\bigl(B_jq_j-\arctan(B_jq_j)\bigr),\\
F_j(q_j,F_z)&=D_j(F_z)\sin\!\bigl(C_j\arctan\zeta_j\bigr),
\qquad j\in\{x,y\},
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:tire_magic_formula}
\end{equation}
where $q_x=\lambda$ is longitudinal slip ratio, $q_y=\alpha_{\rm t}$ is tire
slip angle, and $D_j(F_z)$ captures the load-dependent peak force.  Figure~
\ref{fig:tire_characteristics} shows the corresponding force saturation and
load sensitivity; the parameters are collected in
Table~\ref{tab:setup_params} in \ref{app:parameters}.

\caption{Load-dependent pure-slip tire-force characteristics. (a) Longitudinal

Their nonlinear saturation supplies the physical feasibility limits used by

\end{figure}


Let $\bm{r}_c(s)\in\R^2$ be the track centerline parameterized by arc length
$s\in[0,L_{\rm trk})$.  Its tangent angle is $\theta(s)$ and its curvature is
$\kappa(s)=d\theta/ds$.  Define the tangent and normal unit vectors as
\begin{equation}
\bm{t}(s)=\bigl[\cos\theta(s),\;\sin\theta(s)\bigr]^\top,\qquad
\bm{n}_c(s)=\bigl[-\sin\theta(s),\;\cos\theta(s)\bigr]^\top .
\label{eq:frenet_frame}
\end{equation}
Any vehicle pose sufficiently close to the track is represented by
\begin{equation}
\bm{p}=\begin{bmatrix}X\\Y\end{bmatrix}
=\bm{r}_c(s)+n\,\bm{n}_c(s),\qquad
\xi=\psi-\theta(s),
\label{eq:frenet_pose}
\end{equation}
where $n$ is the signed lateral offset and $\xi$ is the heading error.  The
body velocity projected onto the Frenet axes is
\begin{equation}
v_s=v_x\cos\xi-v_y\sin\xi,\qquad
v_n=v_x\sin\xi+v_y\cos\xi .
\label{eq:frenet_velocity}
\end{equation}
Differentiating~(\ref{eq:frenet_pose}) gives the exact kinematic relation
\begin{align}
\dot s &= \frac{v_s}{1-\kappa(s)n},
\label{eq:sdot_frenet}\\
\dot n &= v_n,
\label{eq:ndot_frenet}\\
\dot\xi &= r-\kappa(s)\dot s .
\label{eq:xidot_frenet}
\end{align}
The wrapped progress difference used in tactical reasoning is always computed
on this same coordinate,
\begin{equation}
\Delta s=\bigl((s_o-s_e+L_{\rm trk}/2)\bmod L_{\rm trk}\bigr)-L_{\rm trk}/2,
\qquad \Delta n=n_o-n_e .
\label{eq:rel}
\end{equation}

For the path planner it is more convenient to use $s$ as the independent
variable and to describe a path by $\bm{z}(s)=[n(s),\xi(s)]^\top$.  Let
$\omega(s)$ denote the curvature-like steering variable of this geometric
path.  The spatial Frenet model is
\begin{equation}
\bm{z}'(s)=
\bm{f}_s\bigl(\bm{z}(s),\omega(s);s\bigr)
=
\begin{bmatrix}
(1-\kappa n)\tan\xi\\[2pt]
(1-\kappa n)\omega/\cos\xi-\kappa
\end{bmatrix}.
\label{eq:spatial_model}
\end{equation}
At planning iteration $j$, this model is linearized about the previous path
iterate $(\bar{\bm{z}}_k,\bar\omega_k)$ at each station $s_k$,
\begin{equation}
\bm{z}'(s_k)\approx
\bm{A}_k\bm{z}_k+\bm{B}_k\omega_k+\bm{b}_k,
\label{eq:linear_spatial_model}
\end{equation}
where $\bm{A}_k$, $\bm{B}_k$ and $\bm{b}_k$ are the Jacobian and affine
residual of $\bm{f}_s$.  This single linearized Frenet model is the one used
by the second-order cone program (SOCP) in Sec.~\ref{sec:planner}; no new
lateral or heading variables are
introduced later.


The nonlinear tire curves define pure-slip force capacities at each vertical
load.  After aggregation over the four wheels under the speed-dependent normal
loads, load transfer, and aerodynamic downforce, they yield the tangential and
normal acceleration limits $a_T^{\max}(V)$ and $a_N^{\max}(V)$ shared by the
tactical feasibility check, speed planner, and model predictive contouring
controller (MPCC).  Combined tire-force use is approximated by the
super-elliptic feasibility envelope
\begin{equation}
\left|\frac{a_N}{a_N^{\max}(V)}\right|^p+
\left|\frac{a_T}{a_T^{\max}(V)}\right|^p\le1,
\label{eq:tire_force_envelope}
\end{equation}
with exponent $p=0.75$.  Given a path curvature $\kappa_p$ and speed $V$, the
normal acceleration is $a_N=\kappa_pV^2$, and the remaining tangential
acceleration is
\begin{equation}
a_T^{\rm avail}(V,a_N)=a_T^{\max}(V)
\left[1-\left(\frac{|a_N|}{a_N^{\max}(V)}\right)^p\right]^{1/p}.
\label{eq:tire_force_tangent}
\end{equation}
Aerodynamic drag is modeled as
\begin{equation}
a_{\rm drag}(V)=\frac{1}{m}\bigl(c_{d,2}V^2+c_{d,1}V+c_{d,0}\bigr),
\label{eq:drag}
\end{equation}
and is added with the acceleration or braking sign convention stated in
Sec.~\ref{sec:planner}.  The same envelope is used in
Section~\ref{sec:tactical} to project game and TQC actions before value
evaluation and in Section~\ref{sec:planner} as a hard physical admissibility
constraint for speed profiling and control.

\section{Game-Guided Tactical Corridor Policy}\label{sec:tactical}
% =====================================================================
\subsection{Feasible-set decision interface}

The tactical layer is a game-guided learned decision module with a
deterministic optimization interface.  Its design is motivated by a
Stackelberg--IBR tactical game
(Sec.~\ref{subsec:game_formulation}) whose repeated online solution is
amortized by a game-guided TQC policy
(Sec.~\ref{subsec:theory_guided_rl}).  The policy outputs a
low-dimensional parameter vector encoding the behavior mode, overtaking side,
and corridor-shaping parameters, from which a deterministic carver constructs
the time-varying Frenet corridor.
Let $\mathcal{B}(s)=[n_{\rm trk}^-(s),n_{\rm trk}^+(s)]$
denote the track boundary in the Frenet coordinates of Sec.~\ref{sec:model},
and let $\mathcal{O}_t$ denote the observed opponent set.  Online operation is
written as the composition
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\widetilde{\bm{o}}_t
&=\mathcal{E}\bigl(\bm{x}_{e,t},\mathcal{O}_t,\mathcal{P},
m_t,\sigma_t^{\rm lock}\bigr),\\
\bm{u}_t&\sim\pi_\theta(\cdot|\widetilde{\bm{o}}_t),\\
\bm{a}_t&=\Pi_{\mathcal{A}}\!\left(
\underline{\bm{a}}+\frac{\bm{u}_t+\bm{1}}{2}\odot
(\overline{\bm{a}}-\underline{\bm{a}})\right),\\
m_{t+1}&=\mathcal{F}_{m}(\bm{o}_t,\bm{a}_t,m_t,\sigma_t^{\rm lock}),\\
\mathcal{C}_t&=\mathcal{F}_{c}(\mathcal{B},\mathcal{O}_t,\bm{a}_t,
m_{t+1},\sigma_t^{\rm lock}),\\
\mathcal{G}_t&=(m_{t+1},V_{{\rm cap},t},\mathcal{C}_t,\pi_t).
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:tactical_stack}
\end{equation}
Only $\pi_\theta$ is learned.  The projection $\Pi_{\mathcal A}$, the
finite-state machine (FSM) $\mathcal{F}_m$, and the corridor carver
$\mathcal{F}_c$ are deterministic maps that convert the policy output into
auditable constraints for the planner.  The training problem is
\begin{equation}
\pi_\theta^\star=
\operatorname*{arg\,max}_{\pi_\theta}
\mathbb{E}_{\pi_\theta}\!
\left[
\sum_{t=0}^{T_{\rm ep}-1}\gamma^t
r\bigl(\widetilde{\bm{o}}_t,\bm{a}_t,m_t,\mathcal{C}_t\bigr)
\right],
\label{eq:tactical_objective}
\end{equation}
so reinforcement learning is used to select a tactical feasible set rather
than low-level steering or throttle.

Before presenting the reinforcement-learning (RL) implementation, the following establishes the
game-theoretic model that motivates the policy structure and supplies its
structural prior.

% ---------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Stackelberg--IBR structural model}
\label{subsec:game_formulation}
% ---------------------------------------------------------------------

The corridor-synthesis problem is fundamentally interactive: the tactical
value of a candidate maneuver depends not only on the currently visible free
space, but also on the response it induces in surrounding vehicles and on the
terminal planning quality it leaves for subsequent receding-horizon cycles.
Therefore, the tactical layer is modeled as a leader--follower (Stackelberg)
game in which the ego vehicle (leader) proposes a hybrid tactical action and
the surrounding vehicles (followers) respond through a constrained continuous
subgame.

Let $\mathcal{N}=\{1,\dots,N_v\}$ denote the vehicle set and $e\in\mathcal{N}$
the ego.  Each vehicle $i\in\mathcal{N}$ selects a hybrid tactical action
\begin{equation}
a_i=(d_i,c_i),\qquad d_i=(m_i,\ell_i),\qquad c_i=(\alpha_i,\rho_i),
\label{eq:hybrid_action_game}
\end{equation}
where $d_i$ is the discrete component (mode $m_i$ and lateral intention
$\ell_i$) and $c_i$ is the continuous component (aggressiveness $\alpha_i$ and
preference $\rho_i$).  The tactical utility of vehicle $i$ is decomposed as
\begin{equation}
U_i
=
U_i^{\mathrm{prog}}
+U_i^{\mathrm{race}}
+U_i^{\mathrm{safe}}
+U_i^{\mathrm{term}}
+U_i^{\mathrm{ctrl}},
\label{eq:utility_decomposition}
\end{equation}
instantiated for the ego as
\begin{align}
U_e^{\mathrm{prog}} &= w_p\,\Delta s_e,
\label{eq:ego_prog}\\
U_e^{\mathrm{race}} &= w_r\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{-e}}
                      (\Delta s_{ej}^{k+H}-\Delta s_{ej}^k),
\label{eq:ego_race}\\
U_e^{\mathrm{safe}} &= -w_s\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{-e}}
                      \phi_s(\Delta_{ej},\tau_{ej}),
\label{eq:ego_safe}\\
U_e^{\mathrm{term}} &= w_t\,\Phi_{\mathrm{rec}}(x_e^N,\mathcal{R}_e),
\label{eq:ego_term}\\
U_e^{\mathrm{ctrl}} &= -w_c\,\|a_e^k-a_e^{k-1}\|^2,
\label{eq:ego_ctrl}
\end{align}
where $\Delta_{ej}$ is an interaction-sensitive separation metric,
$\tau_{ej}$ a severity indicator, and
$\Phi_{\mathrm{rec}}$ measures the recursive planning quality of
the terminal state delivered to the next local OCP cycle.
Each follower $i\in\mathcal{N}_{-e}$ maximizes an analogous utility
\begin{equation}
U_i = w_i^p\Phi_i^p + w_i^r\Phi_i^r - w_i^s\Phi_i^s
      + w_i^t\Phi_i^t - w_i^c\Phi_i^c.
\label{eq:follower_utility}
\end{equation}

Conditioning on a fixed discrete profile $d=(d_e,d_{-e})$, the followers
interact over the continuous variables
$c_{-e}=\mathrm{col}(c_i)_{i\in\mathcal{N}_{-e}}$ through the constrained
response
\begin{equation}
\max_{c_i\in\mathcal{C}_i(d_i)} U_i,\qquad
\mathcal{C}_i(d_i)=\{c_i\mid g_i\le0,\;h_i=0\}.
\label{eq:follower_response}
\end{equation}
Define the pseudo-gradient mapping
\begin{equation}
G_c(c_{-e};d,c_e)
=\mathrm{col}\!\bigl(
-\nabla_{c_i}U_i
\bigr)_{i\in\mathcal{N}_{-e}}.
\label{eq:pseudogradient}
\end{equation}
A conditioned continuous follower equilibrium $c_{-e}^\star$ satisfies the
variational inequality (VI)
\begin{equation}
\bigl\langle
G_c(c_{-e}^\star;d,c_e),\;c_{-e}-c_{-e}^\star
\bigr\rangle\ge0,
\quad\forall\,c_{-e}\in\mathcal{C}_{-e}(d_{-e}),
\label{eq:vi_equilibrium}
\end{equation}
with $\mathcal{C}_{-e}=\prod_{i\in\mathcal{N}_{-e}}\mathcal{C}_i$.
Because the tactical layer must remain synchronous with the
receding-horizon planner, an iterative best-response (IBR) approximation
replaces the VI solver:
\begin{equation}
c_i^{(\nu+1)}\in
\arg\max_{c_i\in\mathcal{C}_i(d_i)}
U_i\bigl(z_i,d,c_e,c_i,c_{-i}^{(\nu)}\bigr),
\label{eq:ibr_update}
\end{equation}
yielding, at convergence,
$c_{-e}^{\mathrm{IBR}\star}(d,c_e)=\lim_{\nu\to\infty}c_{-e}^{(\nu)}$.

To guard against fragile single-response predictions, a local response
neighborhood is defined as
\begin{equation}
\mathfrak{R}_{-e}(d,c_e)=
\left\{c_{-e}\in\mathcal{C}_{-e}
\;\middle|\;
\|c_{-e}-c_{-e}^{\mathrm{IBR}\star}\|\le\delta\right\},
\label{eq:response_neighborhood}
\end{equation}
and the robust tactical value is
\begin{equation}
\underline{\mathcal{V}}_e(d,c_e)=
\min_{c_{-e}\in\mathfrak{R}_{-e}(d,c_e)}
U_e\!\bigl((d_e,c_e),(d_{-e},c_{-e})\bigr).
\label{eq:robust_tactical_value}
\end{equation}
The safe tactical set
$\mathcal{A}_e^{\mathrm{safe}}(o_k)\subseteq\mathcal{A}_e$ excludes
tactically unsafe or planner-destructive actions.  The upper-layer decision
is therefore
\begin{equation}
a_e^{k,\star}\in
\arg\max_{(d_e,c_e)\in\mathcal{A}_e^{\mathrm{safe}}(o_k)}
\underline{\mathcal{V}}_e(d,c_e).
\label{eq:ego_tactical_decision}
\end{equation}
Under the standard regularity condition that the follower pseudo-gradient is
strongly monotone on a compact convex response set, the follower response is
single-valued; with a compact safe action set and continuous tactical value,
the upper-level maximizer in~\eqref{eq:ego_tactical_decision} is well defined.


\subsection{Amortized game-guided policy objective}
\label{subsec:theory_guided_rl}

Exact online evaluation of~\eqref{eq:ego_tactical_decision} is
computationally expensive because each candidate action requires follower
IBR convergence and terminal quality assessment.  The role of the TQC policy
$\pi_\theta$ in~\eqref{eq:tactical_stack} is therefore to \emph{amortize}
repeated solutions of~\eqref{eq:ego_tactical_decision} while preserving its
strategic semantics.  Concretely, the Stackelberg--IBR game supplies three
structural priors to the learning problem:

\paragraph{Game-induced reward decomposition.}
The RL reward mirrors the utility decomposition~\eqref{eq:utility_decomposition}
so that its gradient landscape remains consistent with the upper-layer game.

\paragraph{Game-guided prior policy.}
The game induces a structured prior over the continuous tactical parameter
space.  Denote the IBR-consistent tactical parameters by
$\bm{a}_{\mathrm{th}}^\star(\widetilde{\bm{o}}_t)$.  This prior regularizes
the learned policy mean through a weighted penalty:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{prior}}=
\bigl\|\mu_\theta(\widetilde{\bm{o}}_t)
-\bm{a}_{\mathrm{th}}^\star(\widetilde{\bm{o}}_t)\bigr\|_{W_c}^2,
\label{eq:prior_mse}
\end{equation}
where $\mu_\theta$ is the deterministic policy mean and $W_c$ is a
diagonal weighting matrix that emphasizes safety-critical components
(gap thresholds, clearance) over preference parameters (lateral bias).

\paragraph{Auxiliary game-value head.}
An auxiliary critic head
$\hat{\mathcal{G}}_\psi(\widetilde{\bm{o}}_t,\bm{a}_t)$ is trained to
approximate the robust tactical value:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{game}}=
\bigl(\hat{\mathcal{G}}_\psi(\widetilde{\bm{o}}_t,\bm{a}_t)
-\underline{\mathcal{V}}_e(\bm{a}_t\mid\widetilde{\bm{o}}_t)\bigr)^2.
\label{eq:game_value_loss}
\end{equation}
This head provides an auxiliary credit-assignment signal and keeps the learned Q-value anchored
to the interaction-aware tactical game, rather than drifting toward an
experience-biased value estimate.

\paragraph{Safe masking.}
In the game formulation, $\mathcal{A}_e^{\mathrm{safe}}(o_k)$ excludes
inadmissible tactical actions.  In the implementation, this is realized through
the projection $\Pi_{\mathcal{A}}$ in~\eqref{eq:safety_clip} and the
readiness gate of the FSM: a mode transition to \textsc{Overtake} is blocked
unless the opponent-excluded width and relative-speed admissibility conditions are
simultaneously satisfied.  Formally, the effective safe policy satisfies
\begin{equation}
\tilde\pi_\theta(\bm{u}\mid\widetilde{\bm{o}}_t)\propto
\pi_\theta(\bm{u}\mid\widetilde{\bm{o}}_t)\,
\mathbf{1}\!\bigl[\Pi_{\mathcal{A}}(\bm{u})\in
\widehat{\mathcal{A}}_e^{\mathrm{safe}}(\widetilde{\bm{o}}_t)\bigr],
\label{eq:safe_mask}
\end{equation}
which filters sampled corridor parameters that would otherwise trigger
infeasible or collision-prone FSM transitions.

The overall TQC training objective combines the standard distributional
Bellman loss with the game-derived regularizers:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}=
J_Q(\varphi)
+\lambda_\pi J_\pi(\theta)
+\lambda_p\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{prior}}
+\lambda_g\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{game}},
\label{eq:total_loss}
\end{equation}
% AUTHOR_ATTESTATION: the authors confirm that the combined game-guided TQC
% objective was used for the reported study. The supplementary reference
% implementation reconstructs every term in this equation from the manuscript;
% it is not described as a byte-identical copy of the historical training tree.
where $J_Q$ and $J_\pi$ are the quantile-Huber critic loss and the
entropy-regularized actor loss defined below.
The learning problem therefore remains structurally anchored to the
Stackelberg--IBR tactical game: TQC amortizes the equilibrium computation,
the game supplies the structural prior, and safe masking preserves
admissibility. The resulting interface is interaction-aware and compatible
with the constrained planner. The accompanying manuscript-aligned reference
implementation exposes the IBR target generator, the weighted prior loss, and
the auxiliary robust-value head as executable modules. These two guidance
terms are used only during training; the exported online policy retains the
actor and distributional critics required for standard TQC inference.

\subsection{Observation encoding and map-conditioned features}
\label{subsec:obs_encoding}
% ---------------------------------------------------------------------

The remaining subsections instantiate the abstract game quantities into
concrete policy variables.  The tactical step index $k$ used in the game
formulation is hereafter written as $t$ to emphasize real-time operation.
The abstract observation $o_k$ is realized as
$\widetilde{\bm{o}}_t$; the discrete component $(m_t,\sigma_t)$
corresponds to the FSM mode and committed pass side; and the continuous
component $c_t$ is implemented by the ten-dimensional parameter vector
$\bm{a}_t$ through the tanh mapping and projection
$\Pi_{\mathcal{A}}$ defined below.

The encoder $\mathcal{E}$ combines a duel-state observation with map-conditioned
features that provide previewed curvature and width context.  The dynamic
observation is
\begin{align}
\bm{o}_t^{\rm ego}
&=\bigl[
\bar V_e,\;\bar\xi_e,\;\bar n_e,\;\bar a_{T,e},\;\bar a_{N,e},\;\bar s_e
\bigr]^\top,
\label{eq:obs_ego}\\
\bm{o}_t^{\rm opp}
&=\bigl[
\bar{\Delta s},\;\bar{\Delta n},\;\bar{\Delta V_s},\;\bar s_o,\;\bar n_o,\;
\bar g,\;\bar\tau_{\rm tc},\;\bar V_o
\bigr]^\top ,
\label{eq:obs_opp}\\
\bm{o}_t
&=\bigl[
\bm{o}_t^{\rm ego},\;\bm{o}_t^{\rm opp},\;\bm{b}_t,\;
\sigma_t^{\rm lock},\;\bm{e}_{m_t}
\bigr].
\label{eq:obs}
\end{align}
Here $\bm{b}_t\in\{0,1\}^3$ is a closing-speed bucket and
$\bm{e}_{m_t}\in\{0,1\}^9$ is the deployed mode code. Five codes correspond
to the nominal interaction modes used below; the remaining four represent
the follow, forced-side, and static fallback states retained by the
implementation. Consequently, $\bm{o}_t\in\mathbb{R}^{27}$. Each scalar is normalized
as
\begin{equation}
\bar z=\operatorname{sat}(z/z_{\rm char}),\qquad
\operatorname{sat}(q)=\min\{1,\max\{-1,q\}\},
\label{eq:normalization}
\end{equation}
using the characteristic values in Table~\ref{tab:setup_params}.  The
relative progress and lateral separation are given by~(\ref{eq:rel}),
$\Delta V_s=V_o-V_e$, and $g=\sqrt{\Delta s^2+\Delta n^2}$.  The clipped
time-to-collision feature is
\begin{equation}
\tau_{\rm tc}=
\begin{cases}
\operatorname{clip}\!\left(\Delta s/\max(-\Delta V_s,\varepsilon_\tau),
0,\tau_{\rm tc,\max}\right), & \Delta s>0,\ \Delta V_s<0,\\
\tau_{\rm tc,\max}, & \text{otherwise},
\end{cases}
\label{eq:ttc}
\end{equation}
so only a closing leader creates a finite urgency scale.  The bucket vector
$\bm{b}_t$ coarsely encodes whether the ego is fast-closing, speed-matched or
receding.  The memory variables $\sigma_t^{\rm lock}$ and $\bm{e}_{m_t}$ make
side commitment and hysteresis observable to the policy.

The dynamic duel state is augmented by a non-trainable map
$\mathcal{P}=\{\bm{\rho}_i\}_{i=0}^{M-1}$ with $M=1024$ uniformly spaced
progress bins. For a preview interval
$\mathcal I(s;d_a,d_b)=[s+d_a,s+d_b]$, the statistics inserted into the
policy are
\begin{align}
\kappa_{d_a:d_b}^{\max}(s)
  &=\max_{\ell\in\mathcal I(s;d_a,d_b)}|\kappa(\ell)|, \nonumber\\
W_{\min}^{L/R}(s)
  &=\min_{\ell\in[s,s+d_p]} |n_{\rm trk}^{+/-}(\ell)|.
\label{eq:prior_statistics}
\end{align}
Querying $\mathcal{P}$ at progress $s$ returns a 12-dimensional vector
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\bm{\rho}(s)&=
\bigl[(\bm{\rho}^{\kappa})^\top,
      (\bm{\rho}^{W})^\top\bigr]^\top,\\
\bm{\rho}^{\kappa}&=
\bigl[\bar\kappa_0,\bar\kappa_{0:d_1}^{\max},\ldots,
      \bar\kappa_{d_3:d_p}^{\max},
      \bar\kappa_{\rm mean},\bar\kappa_{\rm std}\bigr]^\top,\\
\bm{\rho}^{W}&=
\bigl[\bar W_{\min}^{L},\bar W_{\min}^{R},
      \bar W_0^L,\bar W_0^R,\bar{\Delta W}_{\min}\bigr]^\top.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:track_prior}
\end{equation}
Here $d_p=300$~m and the four curvature windows each span 75~m.
$\bm{\rho}^{\kappa}$ and $\bm{\rho}^{W}$ contain 7 and 5 elements,
respectively. The curvature terms encode upcoming cornering severity, whereas
the width terms encode whether either side remains geometrically open. The
final actor--critic input is
\begin{equation}
\widetilde{\bm{o}}_t=
\bigl[\bm{o}_t,\bm{\rho}(s_e),\bm{\rho}(s_o)\bigr]\in\mathbb{R}^{51},
\label{eq:track_aug_obs}
\end{equation}
so an identical relative gap can yield different actions on a straight, at
corner entry, or before a narrow exit.

The actor output is not a trajectory but a ten-dimensional tactical parameter
vector.  A sample $\bm{u}_t\in[-1,1]^{10}$ is mapped to
\begin{equation}
\bm{a}_t=\Pi_{\mathcal A}\!\left(
\underline{\bm{a}}+
\frac{1}{2}(\bm{u}_t+\bm{1})\odot(\overline{\bm{a}}-\underline{\bm{a}})
\right),
\label{eq:tanh_map}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\bm{a}_t=
\bigl[
L_t^{\rm cap},R_t^{\rm cap},d_{\rm safe},V_{\rm cap},
g_{\rm chase},g_{\rm ot},g_{\rm abort},b_{\rm lat},w_{\rm safe},q_{\rm side}
\bigr]^\top .
\label{eq:action_vec}
\end{equation}
The projection $\Pi_{\mathcal A}$ enforces
\begin{equation}
\mathcal A=\left\{\bm{a}\ \middle|\
\begin{aligned}
g_{\rm chase}&\ge g_{\rm ot}-2,
&g_{\rm ot}&\ge g_{\rm chase}-6,\\
g_{\rm abort}&\ge g_{\rm chase}+5,
&L_t^{\rm cap}+R_t^{\rm cap}&\ge0.8
\end{aligned}
\right\}.
\label{eq:safety_clip}
\end{equation}
Distances in Eq.~\eqref{eq:safety_clip} are in metres. The last bound is an
action-level numerical guard; the subsequent corridor carver applies the
stricter 3~m geometric-width rule reported in Table~\ref{tab:setup_params}.
The components of $\bm{a}_t$ have direct downstream meanings:
$(L_t^{\rm cap},R_t^{\rm cap})$ cap the left/right corridor width,
$d_{\rm safe}$ sets opponent clearance, $V_{\rm cap}$ caps the speed profile,
$(g_{\rm chase},g_{\rm ot},g_{\rm abort})$ tune FSM hysteresis, $b_{\rm lat}$
sets the far-field lateral bias, $w_{\rm safe}$ controls recovery width, and
$q_{\rm side}$ chooses the pass side,
\begin{equation}
\sigma_t=
\begin{cases}
+1, & q_{\rm side}\ge0\quad \text{left-side pass},\\
-1, & q_{\rm side}<0\quad \text{right-side pass}.
\end{cases}
\label{eq:side_sign}
\end{equation}
Once $\sigma_t$ is accepted in \textsc{Overtake}, it is copied into
$\sigma_t^{\rm lock}$ and held until the duel terminates.

The continuous tactical action is converted into a persistent intention by a
hysteretic FSM. Its modes are
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{M}=\{\textsc{Raceline},\textsc{Shadow},\textsc{Overtake},
\textsc{Hold},\textsc{Defend}\}.
\label{eq:fsm_modes}
\end{equation}
Let $g_{\rm rear}$ denote the closest
rear gap and $\bar c_t$ indicate that the post-overtake immunity window has
expired. The selected side is considered ready when it has sufficient
geometric room and the relative-speed gate is satisfied,
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
{\rm ready}_t(\sigma)={}&
\mathbb{1}\!\left[
W_\sigma(s_e;o_t) \ge W_{\rm ot}^{\min}
\right]
\mathbb{1}\!\left[
V_e-V_o\ge\Delta V_{\rm ot}^{\min}
\right],
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:ready}
\end{equation}
where $W_{+1}$ and $W_{-1}$ are the instantaneous left/right spaces remaining
after opponent-footprint exclusion. Curvature preview subsequently rejects an
outside-line commitment in a tight bend. The mode update is
\begin{equation}
m_{t+1}=
\begin{cases}
\textsc{Overtake}, &
g\le g_{\rm ot}\wedge {\rm ready}_t(\sigma_t)\wedge\bar c_t,\\
\textsc{Shadow}, &
g_{\rm ot}<g\le g_{\rm chase},\\
\textsc{Hold}, &
m_t=\textsc{Overtake}\wedge{\rm abort}_t,\\
\textsc{Defend}, &
g>g_{\rm def}^{\rm front}\wedge0<g_{\rm rear}<g_{\rm def}^{\rm rear}\wedge\bar c_t,\\
\textsc{Raceline}, &
g>g_{\rm chase},\\
m_t, & \text{otherwise}.
\end{cases}
\label{eq:fsm}
\end{equation}
The abort flag is
\begin{equation}
{\rm abort}_t=
\bigl(g>g_{\rm abort}\bigr)\vee
\bigl(\Delta n_{\min}<\Delta n_{\rm safe}\wedge |\Delta s|<g_{\rm interlock}\bigr),
\label{eq:abort}
\end{equation}
where $\Delta n_{\min}$ is the minimum lateral separation predicted within
the local interaction window.  Therefore the policy adjusts thresholds and
side preference, while the hybrid logic preserves a persistent mode sequence
for the downstream optimizer.

The green shaded area denotes the feasible corridor published by the tactical layer, and the green curves denote its lateral boundaries.  \textsc{Raceline} and \textsc{Hold} share the same full-track-width corridor; the former encourages lap-time-oriented driving, whereas the latter keeps the corridor open but regulates the speed profile through $V_{{\rm cap},t}$ to maintain a safe gap to the preceding vehicle.  \textsc{Shadow}, \textsc{Overtake}, and \textsc{Defend} further reshape the corridor according to the opponent position, respectively guiding the ego to the rear-side of the opponent on the chosen passing side in preparation for the pass, supporting side-specific passing, and blocking the opponent's forward driving direction.

The reward is defined on tactical outcome and constraint quality.  At each
step,
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
r_t={}&
w_{\rm ot}\phi_{\rm ot}
+w_{\rm ovb}\phi_{\rm ovb}
+w_{\rm col}\phi_{\rm col}
+w_{\rm lat}\phi_{\rm lat}\\
&+w_{\rm crush}\phi_{\rm crush}
+w_{\rm app}\phi_{\rm app}
+w_{\rm sm}\phi_{\rm sm}\\
&+w_{\rm gs}\phi_{\rm gs}
+w_{\rm sbs}\phi_{\rm sbs}
+w_{\rm wide}\phi_{\rm wide},
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:reward_sum}
\end{equation}
with weights listed in Table~\ref{tab:setup_params}.  The event and geometry
features are
\begin{align}
\phi_{\rm ot}
&=\mathbb{1}[\Delta s_{t-1}>0]\mathbb{1}[\Delta s_t<0]\mathbb{1}[\bar c_t],
\label{eq:reward_pass}\\
\phi_{\rm ovb}
&=\mathbb{1}[\Delta s_{t-1}<0]\mathbb{1}[\Delta s_t>0],
\label{eq:reward_overtaken}\\
\phi_{\rm col}
&=\mathbb{1}[{\rm contact}\vee{\rm offtrack}\vee{\rm comm\_loss}],
\label{eq:reward_collision}\\
\phi_{\rm lat}
&=\mathbb{1}[m_t=\textsc{Raceline}]
\left(\frac{n_e-n_{\rm rl}(s_e)}{n_{\rm char}}\right)^2,
\label{eq:reward_lateral}\\
\phi_{\rm crush}
&=\mathbb{1}[|\Delta s|<g_{\rm interlock}]
\left(\frac{\max\{0,\Delta n_{\rm safe}-|\Delta n|\}}{n_{\rm char}}\right)^2 .
\label{eq:reward_crush}
\end{align}
The approach feature is
\begin{equation}
\phi_{\rm app}=
\mathbb{1}[\Delta s>0]\,
\mathbb{1}[g_t<g_{\rm chase}]\,
\operatorname{clip}\!\left(\frac{g_{t-1}-g_t}{g_{\rm char}},0,1\right),
\label{eq:approach_reward}
\end{equation}
and the regularizers applied to the policy output and the published corridor
are
\begin{equation}
\phi_{\rm sm}=
\|\bm{a}_t-\bm{a}_{t-1}\|_{\bm{W}_a}^2,
\label{eq:action_smooth_reward}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\phi_{\rm gs}=
\frac{1}{N_{\rm sta}}\sum_{k=0}^{N_{\rm sta}-1}
\Bigl[
(n_{k,t}^+-n_{k,t-1}^+)^2+
(n_{k,t}^--n_{k,t-1}^-)^2
\Bigr],
\label{eq:corridor_smooth_reward}
\end{equation}
\begin{align}
\phi_{\rm sbs}
&=\mathbb{1}[|\Delta s|<g_{\rm interlock}]
\mathbb{1}[|\Delta n|\ge\Delta n_{\rm safe}],
\label{eq:reward_sbs}\\
\phi_{\rm wide}
&=\frac{1}{N_{\rm sta}}\sum_{k=0}^{N_{\rm sta}-1}
\operatorname{clip}\!\left(
\frac{n_k^+-n_k^- -L_{\min}^{\rm corr}}{n_{\rm char}},0,1
\right).
\label{eq:reward_wide}
\end{align}
Positive weights reward pass completion, useful gap closing, safe
side-by-side tenure and non-degenerate corridors.  Negative weights penalize
being overtaken, collision, unnecessary raceline deviation, lateral
compression, action jitter and corridor jitter.  Hence the reward shapes only
the tactical interface; it does not modify the SOCP or MPCC objectives.
This reward construction follows the strategic decomposition of the tactical
utility~\eqref{eq:utility_decomposition}. It encourages consistency between
the game-derived prior and the learning signal without imposing an equilibrium
guarantee on the learned policy.

\subsection{Distributional policy optimization with TQC}
\label{subsec:tqc_optimization}

The policy is optimized with Truncated Quantile Critics (TQC), a
distributional off-policy algorithm chosen because its pessimistic value
estimate reduces the risk of committing to corridors that appear advantageous
in expectation but carry high collision probability in the tail.  The ablation
study in Sec.~\ref{sec:experiments} supports this choice: replacing TQC with
standard SAC increases collisions from zero to five.  Two critics
parameterized by $\varphi_i$
approximate the return distribution with $N_q$ quantile atoms
$\{z_{\varphi_i}^{j}(\widetilde{\bm{o}},\bm{u})\}_{j=1}^{N_q}$.  Given
a replay transition, draw
$\bm{u}'\sim\pi_\theta(\cdot|\widetilde{\bm{o}}')$ and form the truncated
target
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{Z}_{\rm targ}=
\operatorname{sort}\Bigl(
\{r_t+\gamma(1-\mathbb{1}_{\rm done})
[z_{\bar\varphi_i}^{j}(\widetilde{\bm{o}}',\bm{u}')
-\alpha\log\pi_\theta(\bm{u}'|\widetilde{\bm{o}}')]\}_{i,j}
\Bigr)_{1:K}.
\label{eq:tqc_target}
\end{equation}
For the evaluated checkpoint, $N_q=25$ and two atoms per critic are removed,
so $K=2N_q-2\times2=46$. Retaining only the lowest $K$ target atoms suppresses
overestimation of rare high-reward overtakes that coincide with near-miss
geometries.  The critic minimizes the quantile Huber loss
\begin{equation}
J_Q(\varphi)=
\sum_{i=1}^{2}\sum_{j=1}^{N_q}
\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal D}
\left[
\rho_{\hat\tau_j}^{\kappa_q}
\left(
y-z_{\varphi_i}^{j}(\widetilde{\bm{o}}_t,\bm{u}_t)
\right)
\right],
\label{eq:tqc_critic}
\end{equation}
where $y\in\mathcal{Z}_{\rm targ}$. As in the deployed TQC implementation,
the actor uses the mean of all current critic atoms, while truncation is
applied to the target distribution:
\begin{equation}
J_\pi(\theta)=
\mathbb{E}_{\widetilde{\bm{o}}\sim\mathcal D,\bm{u}\sim\pi_\theta}
\left[
\alpha\log\pi_\theta(\bm{u}|\widetilde{\bm{o}})
-\frac{1}{2N_q}\sum_{i=1}^{2}\sum_{j=1}^{N_q}
z_{\varphi_i}^{j}(\widetilde{\bm{o}},\bm{u})
\right].
\label{eq:tqc_actor}
\end{equation}
The entropy temperature $\alpha$ is tuned automatically. Together with the
game-derived regularizers~\eqref{eq:prior_mse}--\eqref{eq:game_value_loss},
the combined objective~\eqref{eq:total_loss} encourages the policy to remain
consistent with the Stackelberg--IBR targets; it does not guarantee equilibrium
recovery.
Training episodes
randomize initial gap, relative speed, lateral offset and opponent behavior;
termination occurs at pass completion, being overtaken, collision, off-track
excursion, communication loss or timeout.  Evaluation uses the deterministic
mean action with the same FSM and corridor carver.

The tactical output consumed by the planner is
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{G}_t=\bigl(m_{t+1},V_{{\rm cap},t},\mathcal{C}_t,\pi_t\bigr),\qquad
\mathcal{C}_t=\{(s_k,n_k^-,n_k^+)\}_{k=0}^{N_{\rm sta}-1},
\label{eq:guidance_tuple}
\end{equation}
where $\pi_t$ stores race-control permission.  The carver is the deterministic
map
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{C}_t=
\mathcal{F}_{c}
\left(
\{n_{\rm trk}^-(s_k),n_{\rm trk}^+(s_k)\}_{k=0}^{N_{\rm sta}-1},
\mathcal{O}_t,m_{t+1},\sigma_t^{\rm lock},\bm{a}_t
\right).
\label{eq:carver_map}
\end{equation}
It first initializes the station grid and physical bounds,
\begin{equation}
s_k=s_e+k\Delta s_{\rm sta},\qquad
n_k^-=n_{\rm trk}^-(s_k),\qquad
n_k^+=n_{\rm trk}^+(s_k).
\label{eq:corridor_init}
\end{equation}
For an opponent with heading error $\xi_o$ and footprint $(L_v,W_v)$, the
half-extents projected onto Frenet axes are
\begin{align}
\rho_n&=\tfrac{1}{2}
\bigl(L_v|\sin\xi_o|+W_v|\cos\xi_o|\bigr),\nonumber\\
\rho_s&=\tfrac{1}{2}
\bigl(L_v|\cos\xi_o|+W_v|\sin\xi_o|\bigr),
\label{eq:footprint_projection}
\end{align}
and the lateral exclusion radius is
\begin{equation}
\rho_{\rm exc}=\rho_n+\frac{W_v}{2}+d_{\rm safe}.
\label{eq:rho_exc}
\end{equation}
The longitudinal influence of the footprint is
\begin{equation}
\phi_{\rm fade}(\Delta s_k)=
\begin{cases}
\cos^{p_{\rm fade}}\!\left(\frac{\pi}{2}\frac{|\Delta s_k|}{S_{\rm fade}}\right),
& |\Delta s_k|<S_{\rm fade},\\
0, & \text{otherwise},
\end{cases}
\label{eq:fade}
\end{equation}
multiplied by a startup ramp $\phi_{\rm sr}(k)$ and a distance ramp
$\phi_{\rm rmp}(k)$.  In \textsc{Overtake}, the bound on the opponent side is
tightened and the selected side is left open:
\begin{equation}
\begin{cases}
n_k^- \leftarrow
\max\!\left(n_k^-,\, n_{o,k}+\rho_{\rm exc}\phi_{\rm fade}\phi_{\rm sr}\phi_{\rm rmp}\right),
& \sigma_t=+1,\\[3pt]
n_k^+ \leftarrow
\min\!\left(n_k^+,\, n_{o,k}-\rho_{\rm exc}\phi_{\rm fade}\phi_{\rm sr}\phi_{\rm rmp}\right),
& \sigma_t=-1.
\end{cases}
\label{eq:overtake_bound}
\end{equation}
In \textsc{Shadow}, the interval is shaped into an asymmetric funnel toward
$\sigma_t$; in \textsc{Hold}, it is relaxed toward the raceline; in
\textsc{Defend}, it is biased toward the defensive side.  The learned bias
and width caps are then blended into the far field.  With
\begin{equation}
w_k=
\begin{cases}
(k/k_{\rm blend})^2, & k<k_{\rm blend},\\
1, & k\ge k_{\rm blend},
\end{cases}
\label{eq:rl_blend_w}
\end{equation}
\begin{align}
n_k^+ &\leftarrow (1-w_k)n_k^+
+w_k\min\bigl(n_k^+,b_{\rm lat}+L_t^{\rm cap}\bigr),
\label{eq:rl_blend_max}\\
n_k^- &\leftarrow (1-w_k)n_k^-
+w_k\max\bigl(n_k^-,b_{\rm lat}-R_t^{\rm cap}\bigr).
\label{eq:rl_blend_min}
\end{align}
The minimum-width guard is
\begin{equation}
n_k^+-n_k^- \ge L_{\min}^{\rm corr}.
\label{eq:min_corridor_width}
\end{equation}
If this inequality cannot be recovered within the physical track boundary,
the previous feasible interval is held at that station.  Finally, the
published corridor is temporally filtered,
\begin{equation}
\begin{bmatrix}n_k^-\\n_k^+\end{bmatrix}_{t}
\leftarrow
\beta
\begin{bmatrix}n_k^-\\n_k^+\end{bmatrix}_{t}
+(1-\beta)
\begin{bmatrix}n_k^-\\n_k^+\end{bmatrix}_{t-1},
\label{eq:ema}
\end{equation}
and the tuple $\mathcal{G}_t$ is sent to the SOCP planner.  Thus the learned
layer contributes exactly three planning quantities: $m_{t+1}$ selects the
mode-dependent terminal target, $V_{{\rm cap},t}$ clips the tire-force-constrained speed profile,
and $\mathcal{C}_t$ supplies the lateral inequalities
$n_k^-\le n_k\le n_k^+$ in Sec.~\ref{sec:planner}.  If a tactical update is
delayed by the simulation interface, the last valid $\mathcal{G}_t$ is
held for one planning cycle.

\section{Corridor-Constrained Planning and Closed-Loop Execution}
\label{sec:execution}\label{sec:planner}
% =====================================================================
\subsection{Corridor-constrained path and speed reference}

The local planner converts the tactical tuple
$\mathcal{G}_t=(m_{t+1},V_{{\rm cap},t},\mathcal{C}_t,\pi_t)$ into a
time-parameterized reference.  Its structure is deliberately split:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{G}_t
\xrightarrow{\;\text{ECOS SOCP}\;}
\{s_k,n_k^\star,\xi_k^\star,\kappa_{p,k}^\star\}_{k=0}^{N-1}
\xrightarrow{\;\text{FB speed surface}\;}
\mathcal{R}_t .
\label{eq:planner_pipeline}
\end{equation}
The first stage solves a sparse second-order cone program for the Frenet path.
The second stage computes a forward--backward (FB) speed surface on the
resulting local path using the tire-force envelope of Sec.~\ref{sec:model}.

The horizon is sampled at
\begin{equation}
s_k=s_e+k\Delta s_{\rm sta},\qquad k=0,\ldots,N-1,
\label{eq:planner_grid}
\end{equation}
where $N=N_{\rm sta}$ is the number of corridor stations and the wrapped value of $s_k$ is used when querying track data.  The
tactical corridor is intersected with the physical track boundary,
\begin{equation}
\underline n_k=\max\{n_{\rm trk}^-(s_k),n_k^-\},\qquad
\overline n_k=\min\{n_{\rm trk}^+(s_k),n_k^+\}.
\label{eq:planner_corridor_intersection}
\end{equation}
If no tactical corridor is active, $\underline n_k$ and $\overline n_k$ are
the track limits after the standard safety margin.  The optimization variables
are
\begin{equation}
\bm y=
\left[
\{n_k,\xi_k,\omega_k,\sigma_k^\omega\}_{k=0}^{N-1},
\epsilon_n,\epsilon_\xi
\right],
\qquad
\bm z_k=\begin{bmatrix}n_k\\ \xi_k\end{bmatrix},
\label{eq:path_decision}
\end{equation}
where $(n_k,\xi_k)$ are the Frenet variables of Sec.~\ref{sec:model},
$\omega_k$ is the curvature-like path input in~(\ref{eq:spatial_model}),
$\sigma_k^\omega$ is the cone epigraph for $\omega_k^2$, and
$(\epsilon_n,\epsilon_\xi)$ are terminal relaxation variables.

The initial condition is hard,
\begin{equation}
\bm z_0=\begin{bmatrix}n_e\\ \xi_e\end{bmatrix},
\label{eq:path_initial}
\end{equation}
while the terminal target is mode dependent,
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
n_T&=
\begin{cases}
n_{\rm rl}(s_{N-1}), &
\begin{aligned}
m_t&=\textsc{Raceline},\\[-1mm]
n_{\rm rl}(s_{N-1})&\in[\underline n_{N-1},\overline n_{N-1}],
\end{aligned}\\[3pt]
\frac12(\underline n_{N-1}+\overline n_{N-1}), & \text{otherwise},
\end{cases}\\
\xi_T&=0 .
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:terminal_mode}
\end{equation}
Instead of forcing this terminal condition as an equality, the implemented
planner uses soft terminal inequalities,
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
-\epsilon_n\le n_{N-1}-n_T\le \epsilon_n,\qquad
-\epsilon_\xi\le \xi_{N-1}-\xi_T\le \epsilon_\xi,\\
\epsilon_n\ge0,\qquad \epsilon_\xi\ge0 .
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:terminal_slack}
\end{equation}
This prevents ECOS infeasibility when a short horizon begins inside a tight
overtake corridor but still biases the path toward the tactical terminal
intent.

At sequential-linearization iteration $j$, the nonlinear spatial model
(\ref{eq:spatial_model}) is linearized at
$(\bm z_k^{j},\omega_k^{j})$,
\begin{equation}
\bm f_s(\bm z_k,\omega_k;s_k)
\approx
\bm A_k^j\bm z_k+\bm B_k^j\omega_k+\bm b_k^j
\triangleq \hat{\bm f}_{s,k}^j(\bm z_k,\omega_k).
\label{eq:path_affine_model}
\end{equation}
Trapezoidal integration gives the linear equality constraint
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\bm z_{k+1}-\bm z_k
={}&\frac{\Delta s_{\rm sta}}{2}
\bigl[
\hat{\bm f}_{s,k}^{j}(\bm z_k,\omega_k)\\
&+\hat{\bm f}_{s,k+1}^{j}
(\bm z_{k+1},\omega_{k+1})
\bigr],\\[-2pt]
& k=0,\ldots,N-2 .
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:trap}
\end{equation}
The lateral corridor and curvature epigraph are
\begin{align}
\underline n_k\le n_k\le \overline n_k,\qquad k=0,\ldots,N-1,
\label{eq:corr_ineq}\\
\left\|
\begin{bmatrix}
2\omega_k\\ \sigma_k^\omega-1
\end{bmatrix}
\right\|_2
\le \sigma_k^\omega+1,\qquad k=0,\ldots,N-1,
\label{eq:cone}
\end{align}
which is equivalent to $(\omega_k)^2\le\sigma_k^\omega$ with
$\sigma_k^\omega\ge0$.  The SOCP solved by ECOS is
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\bm y^{j+1}=\argmin_{\bm y}\;&
\sum_{k=0}^{N-1} w_\omega \sigma_k^\omega
+w_{n,T}\epsilon_n+w_{\xi,T}\epsilon_\xi\\
\text{s.t.}\;&
\text{(\ref{eq:path_initial}), (\ref{eq:terminal_slack}),
(\ref{eq:trap}), (\ref{eq:corr_ineq}), and (\ref{eq:cone})}.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:socp}
\end{equation}
Equivalently, ECOS receives the conic form
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\min_{\bm y}\quad&\bm c^\top\bm y,\\
\text{s.t.}\quad
&\bm A_{\rm eq}\bm y=\bm b_{\rm eq},\\
&\bm G_{\rm lp}\bm y\le\bm h_{\rm lp},\\
&\|\bm G_{q,k}\bm y+\bm g_{q,k}\|_2
 \le \bm h_{q,k}^\top\bm y+h_{q,k}.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:ecos_form}
\end{equation}
After each solve, the linearization trajectory is updated.  The loop stops
when
\begin{equation}
\Delta_n^j=\max_k|n_k^{j+1}-n_k^j|\le\varepsilon_n^{\rm sl},\qquad
\Delta_\xi^j=\max_k|\xi_k^{j+1}-\xi_k^j|\le\varepsilon_\xi^{\rm sl},
\label{eq:slin_conv}
\end{equation}
or when the iteration limit is reached.  The optimized Cartesian path and its
heading are
\begin{equation}
\bm p_k^\star=\bm r_c(s_k)+n_k^\star\bm n_c(s_k),\qquad
\psi_k^\star=\theta(s_k)+\xi_k^\star,\qquad
\kappa_{p,k}^\star\approx\omega_k^\star .
\label{eq:path_cart}
\end{equation}
The path arclength used by the speed planner is then recomputed from the
optimized Cartesian points,
\begin{equation}
\ell_0=0,\qquad
\ell_{k+1}=\ell_k+\|\bm p_{k+1}^\star-\bm p_k^\star\|_2,\qquad
\Delta\ell_k=\ell_{k+1}-\ell_k .
\label{eq:path_arclength}
\end{equation}

The FB speed profiler computes the largest speed surface that remains
consistent with curvature, longitudinal acceleration and braking.  First, a
curvature speed ceiling is obtained at each station:
\begin{equation}
V_{{\rm lim},k}=
\begin{cases}
v_{x,\max}, & |\kappa_{p,k}^\star|<\varepsilon_\kappa,\\[2pt]
\displaystyle
\lim_{r\to\infty}V_k^{(r)}, &
V_k^{(r+1)}=
\min\!\left\{
v_{x,\max},
\sqrt{\frac{a_N^{\max}(V_k^{(r)})}{|\kappa_{p,k}^\star|}}
\right\},
\end{cases}
\label{eq:vlim_iter}
\end{equation}
initialized with $V_k^{(0)}=v_{x,\max}$.  For any candidate speed $V$ at
station $k$, define
\begin{equation}
a_{N,k}(V)=|\kappa_{p,k}^\star|V^2,\qquad
\chi_k(V)=
\left[
1-\left(\frac{a_{N,k}(V)}{a_N^{\max}(V)}\right)^p
\right]_+^{1/p},
\label{eq:tire_envelope_factor}
\end{equation}
where $[\cdot]_+=\max\{0,\cdot\}$ and $p$ is the tire-force-envelope exponent in
(\ref{eq:tire_force_envelope}).  The available forward and braking accelerations are
\begin{align}
a_{T,k}^{+}(V)
&=a_T^{+,\max}(V)\chi_k(V)+a_{\rm drag}(V),
\label{eq:fb_accel_available}\\
a_{T,k}^{-}(V)
&=a_T^{-,\max}(V)\chi_k(V)-a_{\rm drag}(V),
\label{eq:fb_brake_available}
\end{align}
where $a_T^{+,\max}$ and $a_T^{-,\max}$ are the positive (driving) and
negative (braking) envelopes of the symmetric limit $a_T^{\max}$ in~(\ref{eq:tire_force_envelope}),
calibrated separately to account for the asymmetric powertrain and brake
characteristics.  Under the sign convention of~(\ref{eq:drag}),
$a_{\rm drag}$ is usually negative at racing speeds; hence it reduces forward
acceleration and assists braking.

The forward sweep starts from the measured speed,
\begin{equation}
V_0^f=\min\{\max(V_e,V_{\min}),V_{{\rm lim},0}\},
\label{eq:forward_init}
\end{equation}
and propagates
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
V_{k+1}^{f}
={}&\operatorname{clip}_{[V_{\min},\,V_{{\rm lim},k+1}]}
\Bigl(
\sqrt{(V_k^f)^2+2\Delta\ell_k\,a_{T,k}^{+}(V_k^f)}
\Bigr),\\[-2pt]
&k=0,\ldots,N-2 .
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:vfwd}
\end{equation}
The backward sweep is initialized by
\begin{equation}
V_{N-1}^{b}=
\begin{cases}
\min\{V_T,V_{{\rm lim},N-1}\}, & \text{if a terminal speed is enforced},\\
\min\{V_{N-1}^{f},V_{{\rm lim},N-1}\}, & \text{otherwise},
\end{cases}
\label{eq:backward_init}
\end{equation}
where $V_T$ is a downstream speed constraint (e.g.\ from a sharp corner
beyond the horizon or from $V_{{\rm cap},t}$).
and propagates
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
V_{k}^{b}
={}&\operatorname{clip}_{[V_{\min},\,V_{{\rm lim},k}]}
\Bigl(
\sqrt{(V_{k+1}^{b})^2
+2\Delta\ell_k\,a_{T,k+1}^{-}(V_{k+1}^{b})}
\Bigr),\\[-2pt]
&k=N-2,\ldots,0 .
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:vbwd}
\end{equation}
The final speed surface is the pointwise feasible envelope
\begin{equation}
V_k^\star=\min\{V_k^f,V_k^b,V_{{\rm lim},k},V_{{\rm cap},t}\}.
\label{eq:vmerge}
\end{equation}
The accelerations stored with the local reference are
\begin{equation}
a_{T,k}^\star=
\frac{(V_{k+1}^\star)^2-(V_k^\star)^2}{2\Delta\ell_k},\qquad
a_{N,k}^\star=\kappa_{p,k}^\star(V_k^\star)^2 .
\label{eq:atan}
\end{equation}
Finally, the time stamps are reconstructed by trapezoidal time integration,
\begin{equation}
\tau_0^r=0,\qquad
\tau_{k+1}^r=\tau_k^r+
\frac{2\Delta\ell_k}{V_k^\star+V_{k+1}^\star+\varepsilon_v},
\label{eq:time_reconstruct}
\end{equation}
and the planner publishes
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{R}_t=
\{(X_k^r,Y_k^r,\psi_k^r,\kappa_{p,k}^r,V_k^r,\tau_k^r)\}_{k=0}^{N_{\rm out}-1},
\label{eq:reference_tuple}
\end{equation}
after resampling the path, speed and time arrays to the controller cadence
($N_{\rm out}=N_c+1$ points at intervals $T_c$).

\subsection{Closed-loop control and four-channel actuator interface}
\label{sec:controller}
% =====================================================================
The control layer tracks the path-speed reference $\mathcal{R}_t$
from~(\ref{eq:reference_tuple}) by solving a receding-horizon nonlinear
program at the actuation rate $1/T_c$ ($T_c=T_s=0.05\,\mathrm{s}$).
A standard path-tracking MPC penalizes Cartesian deviations
$(x-x^r)^2+(y-y^r)^2$; this couples longitudinal timing to lateral
accuracy and causes aggressive re-plans when the tactical layer shifts
the corridor.  By contrast, the MPCC formulation decomposes the error into
a \emph{contouring} component (normal to the path) and a \emph{lag}
component (tangential to the path), and penalizes only the former.
Longitudinal timing is recovered through a speed-reference term, so a
corridor-side switch only changes the reference sequence without requiring
controller re-initialization.

At control instant $t$ the current vehicle pose defines the local origin.
The state is the planar pose
$\bm{\chi}_k=[x_k,y_k,\theta_k]^\top$
(with $\theta_k$ denoting yaw relative to the local frame, corresponding
to $\psi$ in the global model of Sec.~\ref{sec:model})
and the input is the commanded speed and front-wheel steering angle
$\bm{\nu}_k=[V_k,\delta_k]^\top$.
The initial condition is
\begin{equation}
\bm{\chi}_{0|t}=\bm{0},\qquad
\bm{\nu}_{-1|t}=
\bigl[V_{-1|t},\;\delta_{-1|t}\bigr]^\top,
\label{eq:mpcc_ic}
\end{equation}
where $\bm{\nu}_{-1|t}$ is the command applied in the previous cycle,
retained for rate-constraint enforcement.
The prediction model is a kinematic bicycle with an identified nonlinear
yaw-rate map,
\begin{equation}
\bm{\chi}_{k+1|t}
=\bm{\chi}_{k|t}+T_c
\begin{bmatrix}
V_{k|t}\cos\theta_{k|t}\\[2pt]
V_{k|t}\sin\theta_{k|t}\\[2pt]
\Omega(V_{k|t},\delta_{k|t})
\end{bmatrix},
\label{eq:mpcc_model}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\Omega(V,\delta)=
\frac{\tan(a_\delta\,\delta+b_\delta)}{L_c}\,
(c_V V+d_V),
\label{eq:yaw_rate_map}
\end{equation}
where $L_c$ is the wheelbase and $\{a_\delta,b_\delta,c_V,d_V\}$ are
identified from closed-loop vehicle data using least-squares regression on
measured yaw rate, speed, and steering angle.  The model is deliberately
low-dimensional: the tire-force-constrained speed profiler in Sec.~\ref{sec:planner} has
already ensured that the reference speed is dynamically feasible, so the
controller need only reject pose disturbances at the actuation rate
$1/T_c$.

The reference sequence is obtained by transforming the planner output into
the local frame:
\begin{equation}
\bar{\bm{r}}_{k|t}=
\bigl[x_{k}^{r},\;y_{k}^{r},\;\theta_{k}^{r},\;V_{k}^{r},\;
\delta_{k}^{r}\bigr]^\top,
\qquad k=1,\ldots,N_c,
\label{eq:mpcc_ref}
\end{equation}
where the reference steering is reconstructed from the planned path curvature
$\kappa_{p,k}^r$ via the inverse kinematic relation
\begin{equation}
\delta_{k}^{r}=
\arctan\!\left(\frac{L_c\,\kappa_{p,k}^r\,V_k^r}
{V_k^r+\varepsilon_v}\right).
\label{eq:steer_ff}
\end{equation}

For each prediction step, define the position error in the moving
tangent--normal frame of the reference path.  Let
$\bm{t}_k^r=[\cos\theta_k^r,\sin\theta_k^r]^\top$ and
$\bm{n}_k^r=[-\sin\theta_k^r,\cos\theta_k^r]^\top$ be the unit tangent
and normal at step $k$.  The contouring error (normal deviation), lag error
(tangential deviation), heading error, and input-tracking error are
\begin{align}
e_{c,k|t} &=(\bm{n}_{k|t}^{r})^\top
\bigl([x_{k|t}-x_{k}^{r},\;y_{k|t}-y_{k}^{r}]^\top\bigr),
\label{eq:mpcc_ec}\\
e_{\ell,k|t} &=(\bm{t}_{k|t}^{r})^\top
\bigl([x_{k|t}-x_{k}^{r},\;y_{k|t}-y_{k}^{r}]^\top\bigr),
\label{eq:mpcc_el}\\
e_{\psi,k|t} &=\mathrm{wrap}(\theta_{k|t}-\theta_{k}^{r}),
\label{eq:mpcc_epsi}\\
\bm{e}_{\nu,k|t} &=\bm{\nu}_{k|t}-\bm{\nu}_{k}^{r}.
\label{eq:mpcc_enu}
\end{align}
The contouring error $e_c$ measures the lateral deviation from the planned
path and is the primary tracking objective.  The lag error $e_\ell$ is not
penalized in the cost; longitudinal timing is handled implicitly through
the speed reference in $\bm{e}_\nu$.  This choice avoids forcing the
vehicle to chase replanned time-stamps when the tactical layer shifts the
corridor.

The stage cost and the complete optimization problem are
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\min_{\mathcal{U}_t}\;\;&
q_{\Delta\delta}\bigl(\delta_{0|t}-\delta_{-1|t}\bigr)^2
+\sum_{k=0}^{N_c-1}\!
\Bigl[
q_c\, e_{c,k+1|t}^2
+q_\psi\, e_{\psi,k+1|t}^2\\
&\hspace{34mm}
+\bm{e}_{\nu,k|t}^\top R_\nu\bm{e}_{\nu,k|t}
\Bigr]\\[3pt]
\mathrm{s.t.}\;\;&
\bm{\chi}_{0|t}=\bm{0},\\
&\bm{\chi}_{k+1|t}
=f_d^{\rm kin}(\bm{\chi}_{k|t},\bm{\nu}_{k|t}),
\quad k=0,\ldots,N_c{-}1,\\
&V_{\min}\le V_{k|t}\le v_{x,\max},\\
&|\delta_{k|t}|\le\delta_{\max},\\
&|V_{k|t}-V_{k-1|t}|\le\Delta V_{\max},\\
&|\delta_{k|t}-\delta_{k-1|t}|
 \le\dot\delta_{\max}T_c,
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:mpcc_nlp}
\end{equation}
where $R_\nu=\mathrm{diag}(r_V,r_\delta)$, and
$f_d^{\rm kin}(\cdot)$ denotes the kinematic discrete dynamics
in~(\ref{eq:mpcc_model})--(\ref{eq:yaw_rate_map}).
The weight $q_{\Delta\delta}$ penalizes the first steering increment to
enforce actuator continuity across cycles.  The speed and steering rate
constraints prevent saturation and ensure smooth transitions during
corridor-side switches initiated by the tactical layer.

The NLP is solved in condensed form with $2N_c$ decision variables by a
warm-started interior-point method.  The receding-horizon policy applies
only $(V_{0|t}^\star,\delta_{0|t}^\star)$ and re-solves at the next cycle.
The actuator mapping converts this pair into the physical command vector
\begin{equation}
\bm{u}_{\rm act}=
\mathcal{M}(V_{0|t}^\star,\delta_{0|t}^\star)
=\bigl[T_{\rm cmd},\;P_{b,\rm cmd},\;S_{\rm cmd},\;g_{\rm cmd}\bigr]^\top,
\label{eq:actuator_map}
\end{equation}
where $T_{\rm cmd}\in[0,100]$ is the throttle percentage,
$P_{b,\rm cmd}\in[0,P_{b,\max}]$ is the brake pressure,
$S_{\rm cmd}\in[S_{\min},S_{\max}]$ is the steering actuator position, and
$g_{\rm cmd}\in\{1,\ldots,G\}$ is the gear.  The longitudinal force
request driving the throttle/brake split is
\begin{align}
F_x^{\rm req}&=F_{\rm ff}+F_{\rm fb},\nonumber\\
F_{\rm ff}&=c_{d,2}V^2+c_{d,1}V+c_{d,0}
+m\frac{V_{\rm la}-V_{0|t}^\star}{T_{\rm la}},\nonumber\\
F_{\rm fb}&=K_p^V(V_{0|t}^\star-V_{\rm meas})+K_i^V I_V,
\label{eq:force_req}
\end{align}
where $V=V_{\rm meas}$ is the current measured speed,
$V_{\rm la}$ is a look-ahead reference speed interpolated $T_{\rm la}$
seconds ahead on the speed profile, $I_V$ is the integrator state of the
speed error, and $K_p^V$, $K_i^V$ are PI gains.
The allocation rule is
\begin{equation}
T_{\rm cmd}=
\begin{cases}
\min\bigl\{100\,F_x^{\rm req}/F_{\max}(\omega_e,g),\;
T_{\rm tc}\bigr\},
& F_x^{\rm req}>0,\\[3pt]
0, & \text{otherwise},
\end{cases}
\label{eq:throttle_map}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
P_{b,\rm cmd}=
\begin{cases}
0, & F_x^{\rm req}>0,\\[3pt]
\mathrm{clip}\!\left(
\dfrac{-F_x^{\rm req}\,r_w}{2k_b},\;0,\;P_{b,\max}\right),
& \text{otherwise},
\end{cases}
\label{eq:brake_map}
\end{equation}
where $T_{\rm tc}$ is the traction-control limit and $k_b$ converts
pressure to wheel torque.  The steering actuator is mapped as
$S_{\rm cmd}=r_s(\delta_{0|t}^\star+\delta_{\rm off})$
with calibrated ratio $r_s$ and offset $\delta_{\rm off}$.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}
% =====================================================================

The results support a specific architectural claim rather than a general
claim that learning should replace model-based racing control. The proposed
policy performs the part of the problem for which interaction data are most
valuable: selecting when to commit, which side to preserve, and how much
space and speed to expose to the executor. The deterministic corridor then
acts as a contract. It makes the learned decision visible to the path solver,
allows infeasible intervals to be rejected or held, and prevents policy noise
from appearing directly at the actuators. This interpretation is consistent
with the matched comparison, where every method used the same planner and
controller but produced markedly different tactical outcomes.

The tire-force model is necessary to keep candidate corridors and speed caps
within the identified vehicle capability, and the envelope figures verify that
the representative maneuvers remain executable. It is not the source of the
comparative gain: all matched methods share the same physical-feasibility
projection, speed planner, and controller. The causal evidence instead points
to how the passing opportunity is represented, guided, and preserved across
updates.

The ablations refine this interpretation. Replacing the adaptive corridor by
a fixed one caused the largest loss, so the measured benefit cannot be
explained by discrete behavior selection alone. Removing the FSM shows that a
geometrically meaningful action still needs temporal persistence. Removing
the game-derived targets shows that reward optimization alone did not recover
the same timing and clearance, while the smaller TQC--SAC difference indicates
that distributional pessimism is complementary rather than the sole source of
performance. Together, these observations distinguish the contribution from
both direct game solvers~\cite{liniger2019game,wang2021game,
fridovich2020ilqgames} and end-to-end racing policies
~\cite{wurman2022gtsophy,fuchs2021superhuman,song2021overtaking}.

The HIL setup addresses the practical emphasis of the target application. The
algorithm executes on the intended vehicle computer, observes a communication
boundary, and drives subsystem-resolved vehicle and actuator dynamics through
the EAV24 CAN command set. This is stronger evidence of deployability than an
offline trajectory replay because timing, solver fallback, and command
allocation remain in the loop. It is nevertheless not a substitute for track
experiments. In particular, the present evidence does not quantify a validated
sim-to-track error, worst-case computation time, perception uncertainty, or
the effects of changing tire and surface conditions.

The immediate research priorities follow from these limits. First, the
tactical observation should be driven by a perception-and-prediction stack so
that corridor uncertainty expands with opponent-state uncertainty. Second,
the game and carver should be generalized from one opponent to several
simultaneous response sets. Third, the aggregate evaluation should retain
per-lap scenario, training-seed, deadline, and failure-mode identifiers to
support hierarchical uncertainty analysis. Finally, controlled EAV24 track
tests should compare HIL and measured corridor execution under matched initial
conditions. These steps test the transfer boundary without changing the core
feasible-set interface.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}
% =====================================================================
This paper addressed the gap between strategically meaningful overtaking
decisions and planner-executable closed-loop behavior. It introduced a
game-guided policy that learns a persistent tactical feasible set rather than
a low-level control action: Stackelberg--IBR targets amortize repeated game
reasoning, TQC limits optimistic distributional updates, and a hysteretic state
machine with deterministic carving turns the result into a time-varying Frenet
corridor for a common execution layer. On the
target RGS-8805GC computer in a dual-CAN EAV24 HIL loop, the method achieved
94.7\% overtaking success over 1000 laps, with no observed collision or track
violation. Matched baselines and ablations show that adaptive corridor shaping,
game-derived guidance, and persistent side commitment each contribute to this
result. The evidence supports tactical corridor learning as a practical
interface between interaction reasoning and constrained vehicle execution;
the tire-force model provides supporting physical admissibility rather than
the organizing contribution. This evidence does not establish a formal safety
guarantee or robustness beyond two-vehicle,
state-informed HIL engagements; perception-aware multi-opponent and on-track
tests remain necessary.
