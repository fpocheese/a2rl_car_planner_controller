\begin{abstract}
Overtaking planning and decision-making are central to performance in autonomous racing. 
Limited maneuvering space on narrow closed circuits makes high-speed overtaking difficult and increases the risk of failed passes and collisions. 
% To address this problem, we formulate interactive overtaking as robust tactical feasible-set selection and develop a game-guided decision framework that distills Stackelberg response-set reasoning into persistent tactical corridors for constrained motion planning and closed-loop execution.
To address this problem, we formulate overtaking as robust tactical feasible-set selection and develop a Stackelberg-supervised policy that translates response-set reasoning into persistent tactical corridors for constrained planning and closed-loop control.
First, a robust Stackelberg response-set teacher provides action-prior and action-conditioned robust-value supervision to a Truncated Quantile Critics (TQC) policy that learns passing-side intention, corridor parameters, and speed demand.
Second, a hysteretic state machine preserves the selected passing side, while a deterministic corridor carver combines the learned corridor parameters with the evolving track and interaction geometry to construct a time-varying Frenet corridor.
Finally, a corridor-constrained sequential second-order cone programming (SOCP) planner and a speed profiler generate a continuous path--speed reference, which is tracked using reference-conditioned contouring-error nonlinear model predictive control (RC-NMPC) to produce closed-loop vehicle commands.
Hardware-in-the-loop experiments were conducted using an EAV24 formula race-car simulator. Across 1000 laps, the method achieved an overtaking success rate of 94.7\% and a mean overtaking time of 4.82 s. Compared with three baselines using the same execution stack, the success rate was 31.2–66.2 percentage points higher and the mean overtaking time was 3.01--6.41~s shorter; no collisions or track-boundary violations were observed.

\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
% =====================================================================
Autonomous racing provides a demanding testbed for integrated perception, decision-making, planning, and control under extreme operating conditions. Competitions such as the Indy Autonomous Challenge and the Abu Dhabi Autonomous Racing League (A2RL) have extended this testbed to full-scale head-to-head racing near the limits of vehicle handling~\cite{betz2022survey,hoffmann2026a2rl}. In these races, the ability to complete overtaking maneuvers safely and efficiently directly affects competitive performance. Overtaking is especially difficult on narrow closed circuits, where high speed and limited lateral space shorten the passing window and increase collision risk. A viable overtaking decision must retain its passing-side intent across repeated replanning and leave sufficient maneuvering freedom for downstream path generation and vehicle control. The central challenge is therefore to translate interactive tactical reasoning into a geometrically explicit and temporally persistent feasible region throughout the overtaking maneuver.


\subsection{Related work}

Research on overtaking in autonomous racing has developed along four main
directions: hierarchical maneuver planning, explicit game-theoretic planning,
learning-based decision-making, and hybrid learning--optimization
architectures.
In hierarchical architectures, a tactical layer selects among following,
defending, and passing behaviors, while a lower layer converts the selected
behavior into a path and speed profile.
Finite-state and candidate-trajectory planners provide a structured basis for
real-time computation and allow racing rules, collision checks, and vehicle
constraints to be handled by dedicated modules
~\cite{sureshbabu2022autopass,kim2023hierarchical}.
This modular structure is also adopted in full-scale head-to-head racing
systems, where opponent prediction and local planning operate under tight
real-time constraints~\cite{jung2023iac,hoffmann2026a2rl}.
Within such a hierarchy, Thakkar et al. formulated the high-level decision as
a discrete racing game that generates target waypoints for either a multi-agent
reinforcement-learning controller or a linear-quadratic Nash-game controller
~\cite{thakkar2024hierarchical}.
These approaches provide interpretable tactical logic and a practical route to
real-time implementation. Their tactical outputs, however, are commonly
represented by maneuver states, target waypoints, or selected trajectories and
provide only limited continuous parameterization of the spatial extent
available to downstream planning.

Explicit game-theoretic planners model the coupling between competitors more
directly. Non-cooperative dynamic games jointly evaluate progress, collision
avoidance, blocking, and overtaking, producing behaviors such as shielding,
faking, and opportunistic passing that are difficult to obtain from independent
trajectory optimization~\cite{liniger2019game,wang2021game}. 
Iterative linear-quadratic approximations reduce the computational cost of
solving nonlinear multi-player games, although their local solution quality
remains sensitive to the interaction model and initialization
~\cite{fridovich2020ilqgames}.
Hierarchical Stackelberg formulations separate
the leader's maneuver choice from the follower's response, while payoff
learning adapts the interaction objective from observed behavior
~\cite{ji2023hierarchicalgame,lopez2022payoff}. Level-$K$ reasoning offers a
bounded model of opponent rationality; trajectory mixing can further hedge
against changes in the inferred reasoning level~\cite{ji2024competition}.
Constrained-potential and near-potential formulations instead exploit common
structure in the players' objectives to reduce the online game-solving burden
~\cite{jia2023bayesian,kalaria2025alpha}.  
Collectively, these methods make strategic coupling explicit. Despite
computational approximations, repeatedly solving the game online remains
demanding under the real-time constraints of autonomous racing.

Learning-based methods replace repeated online search with fixed-cost policy
inference and acquire interaction patterns directly from competitive
experience. 
Curriculum learning has produced overtaking policies by gradually
increasing opponent difficulty, while large-scale deep reinforcement learning
has demonstrated high-performance racing and human-like tactical behavior in
simulation~\cite{song2021overtaking,fuchs2021superhuman,wurman2022gtsophy}.
Comparisons with optimal control show that reinforcement learning can exploit
complex racing dynamics effectively, although its behavior depends strongly on
the training distribution and reward design~\cite{lin2023limit}. 
Other learning-based designs condition policies on nominal trajectories or
infer actions from local LiDAR observations
~\cite{evans2023trajectory,hell2024lidar}. Data-driven predictive planners use
learned opponent forecasts to generate overtaking trajectories
~\cite{baumann2025predictive}.
Game-informed deep reinforcement learning couples the
policy reward to another vehicle's action, whereas dynamic-graph and
multi-agent formulations encode pairwise relations or coordinated behavior
among several vehicles~\cite{yuan2022drlgame,hu2023dynamicgraph,
han2024marl}. 
Human feedback provides another source of policy guidance for stabilizing
learned driving strategies~\cite{guo2025rlhf}.
These methods improve interaction awareness. Direct-control policies and
single-trajectory outputs, however, leave limited freedom for constraint-aware
replanning as the interaction evolves during overtaking.

Hybrid learning--optimization architectures therefore expose an intermediate
decision to a model-based planner. Reiter et al. used reinforcement learning to
parameterize the objective of a nonlinear predictive planner, confining
exploration to trajectories accepted by the optimizer
~\cite{reiter2023hierarchical}. In road-traffic applications, learned tactical
layers have been used to anticipate disruptive lane changes and to coordinate
merging sequences subsequently realized by optimal control
~\cite{liu2023proactive,muzahid2025merging}. 
Most closely related to corridor-based planner interfaces, Yang et al. used
MARL-generated cooperative reference trajectories to construct spatio-temporal
corridors for downstream optimization~\cite{yang2026corridor}. Toschi et al.
constructed alternative drivable tunnels from the predicted occupancies of
other vehicles, selected a tunnel according to the current interaction, and
optimized a trajectory within it~\cite{toschi2025modular}.
Across these approaches, intermediate outputs parameterize the planning
objective, provide a guiding trajectory, or select among separately generated
corridors; the corridor parameters themselves are not learned as tactical
outputs.

In summary, despite substantial progress, maintaining a consistent overtaking
intent while preserving sufficient geometric freedom for constrained
replanning remains challenging throughout a high-speed overtaking maneuver.
This motivates a decision framework that represents this intent as a persistent
tactical feasible region and communicates game-supervised tactical decisions
to the planner through side-locked Frenet bounds and speed guidance.

\subsection{Contributions and paper organization}

To address this challenge, this study formulates interactive overtaking as
robust tactical feasible-set selection and develops a game-guided
tactical-corridor decision framework. The framework converts robust Stackelberg
response-set reasoning into a persistent tactical feasible region and speed
guidance for constrained planning and closed-loop control. Specifically, the
main contributions are as follows:

\begin{enumerate}

\item \textbf{Robust Stackelberg Response-Set Supervision:}
We develop a robust Stackelberg response-set teacher that evaluates constraint-admissible tactical candidates against near-best follower responses. The teacher then derives a response-set-optimal action prior and action-conditioned robust-value labels, thereby supervising a Truncated Quantile Critics (TQC) actor--critic to learn robust overtaking decisions.

\item \textbf{Persistent Tactical Feasible-Region Interface:}
We introduce a persistent tactical feasible-region interface that converts TQC outputs into a side-locked, time-varying Frenet corridor with speed guidance. 
A hysteretic state machine and deterministic opponent-footprint carving adapt the feasible region to evolving track and interaction geometry, enabling continuous support for path planning via sequential second-order cone programming (SOCP) and reference-conditioned contouring-error nonlinear model predictive control (RC-NMPC) throughout overtaking.


\item \textbf{System Validation under High-Speed Conditions:}
We deploy the complete decision--planning--control stack on the target onboard computer and evaluate it using an EAV24 hardware-in-the-loop platform under high-speed head-to-head racing conditions. Matched baseline comparisons and ablation studies are also conducted. The results demonstrate a high overtaking success rate and collision-free closed-loop operation, highlighting the framework's potential for practical deployment in autonomous racing.

\end{enumerate}

\section{Problem description and modeling}
\label{sec:model}
% =====================================================================
Figure~\ref{fig:vehicle_track_model} shows the two-vehicle overtaking problem
considered in this study, in which the ego race car must overtake a
non-cooperative opponent on a closed track. The following
models define the vehicle and interaction quantities used by the tactical
policy and executor. Both vehicles are represented by identical rectangular
footprints centered at their state reference points, with length $L_j=5.2$~m
and width $W_j=1.9$~m,
$j\in\{e,o\}$.

For vehicle $i\in\{e,o\}$, $(X_i,Y_i,\psi_i)$ denotes the inertial
pose and $(v_{x,i},v_{y,i},r_i)$ the body-frame velocity and yaw rate, with
$V_i=\sqrt{v_{x,i}^2+v_{y,i}^2}$ and
$\beta_i=\operatorname{atan2}(v_{y,i},v_{x,i})$. Let
$\mathcal W=\{\mathrm{fl},\mathrm{fr},\mathrm{rl},\mathrm{rr}\}$, and let
$\bm r_q=[x_q,y_q]^\top$ and $\delta_q$ denote the body-frame position and
steering angle of wheel $q$. Wheel-level quantities are vehicle specific, with
the vehicle index omitted for clarity. The front wheels share the commanded
steering angle and the rear wheels are unsteered. The planar four-wheel
dynamics are
\begin{equation}
\begin{bmatrix}
\dot X_i\\
\dot Y_i\\
\dot\psi_i\\
\dot v_{x,i}\\
\dot v_{y,i}\\
\dot r_i
\end{bmatrix}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
v_{x,i}\cos\psi_i-v_{y,i}\sin\psi_i\\
v_{x,i}\sin\psi_i+v_{y,i}\cos\psi_i\\
r_i\\
\begin{gathered}
\displaystyle\frac{1}{m}\sum_{q\in\mathcal W}
(F_{x,q}\cos\delta_q-F_{y,q}\sin\delta_q)+r_iv_{y,i}\\[-1mm]
\displaystyle{}-\frac{F_{\rm drag}(V_i)}{m}
\end{gathered}\\[3mm]
\displaystyle\frac{1}{m}\sum_{q\in\mathcal W}
(F_{x,q}\sin\delta_q+F_{y,q}\cos\delta_q)-r_iv_{x,i}\\[3mm]
\begin{gathered}
\displaystyle\frac{1}{I_z}\sum_{q\in\mathcal W}
\bigl[x_q(F_{x,q}\sin\delta_q+F_{y,q}\cos\delta_q)\\[-1mm]
\displaystyle{}-y_q(F_{x,q}\cos\delta_q-F_{y,q}\sin\delta_q)\bigr]
\end{gathered}
\end{bmatrix}.
\label{eq:vehicle_dynamics}
\end{equation}
Here $m$ and $I_z$ are the vehicle mass and yaw inertia, and
$F_{\rm drag}$ is the aerodynamic drag force. The identified pure-slip tire
forces are represented by

\begin{figure}[!t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{model_shuoming_corrected.pdf}
\caption{Vehicle motion and relative geometry in the Frenet frame.}
\label{fig:vehicle_track_model}
\end{figure}

\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
F_{d,q}(\chi_{d,q},F_{z,q})
&=D_d(F_{z,q})\sin\!\bigl(C_d\arctan(B_d\chi_{d,q})\bigr),\\
D_d(F_{z,q})&=F_{z,q}\!\left[\mu_d+s_d
\frac{F_{z,q}-F_{z,0}}{F_{z,0}}\right],\\
B_d&=\frac{\tan\!\left(\pi/(2C_d)\right)}{q_{d,\rm pk}}.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:tire_force_model}
\end{equation}
Here $\chi_{x,q}=\lambda_q$ and $\chi_{y,q}=\alpha_q$. The resultant wheel
forces determine the path-tangential and path-normal accelerations $a_T$ and
$a_N$. Following \cite{veneri2020free}, their admissible combinations are
represented by the speed-interpolated envelope
\begin{equation}
\mathcal A_{\rm tire}(V)
=\{(a_T,a_N):\Phi_{\rm tire}(a_T,a_N,V)\le1\}.
\label{eq:tire_accel_envelope}
\end{equation}
Here $\Phi_{\rm tire}$ is the normalized utilization returned by the
interpolated map, with $\Phi_{\rm tire}=1$ defining the envelope boundary.
The stationwise traction and braking magnitudes used in path planning are
denoted by $a_{T,k}^{+}$ and $b_{T,k}^{-}$, respectively.

Let $\bm r_c(s)$ denote the track centerline parameterized by arc length
$s\in[0,L_{\rm trk})$, with tangent angle $\theta(s)$ and curvature
$\kappa(s)=d\theta/ds$, and let $\bm t(s)$ and $\bm n_c(s)$ be its unit tangent
and normal. With $\bm p_i=[X_i,Y_i]^\top$, the Frenet coordinates satisfy
$\bm p_i=\bm r_c(s_i)+n_i\bm n_c(s_i)$ and
$\xi_i=\psi_i-\theta(s_i)$. Defining the path-aligned velocity components as
$v_{s,i}=V_i\cos(\xi_i+\beta_i)$ and
$v_{n,i}=V_i\sin(\xi_i+\beta_i)$ gives
\begin{equation}
\begin{bmatrix}\dot s_i\\\dot n_i\\\dot\xi_i\end{bmatrix}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
v_{s,i}/(1-\kappa(s_i)n_i)\\
v_{n,i}\\
r_i-\kappa(s_i)v_{s,i}/(1-\kappa(s_i)n_i)
\end{bmatrix}.
\label{eq:frenet_kinematics}
\end{equation}
The lateral coordinate is positive to the left, with
$n_{\rm trk}^{-}(s)\le n\le n_{\rm trk}^{+}(s)$.
Centerline projection gives the relative ego--opponent state
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\Delta s&=\operatorname{wrap}_{L_{\rm trk}}(s_o-s_e),\\
\Delta n&=n_o-n_e,\qquad \Delta v=v_{s,e}-v_{s,o}.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:rel}
\end{equation}
Here $\operatorname{wrap}_{L_{\rm trk}}(\cdot)$ returns the signed arc-length
difference in $[-L_{\rm trk}/2,L_{\rm trk}/2)$; $\Delta s>0$ denotes an
opponent ahead and $\Delta v>0$ denotes the ego closing. The first sampled
instant satisfying $\Delta s<0$ is termed the \emph{Pass} event throughout
this paper.

For path generation, the same geometry is written in spatial form. Here $s$
is the independent variable, $\bm z(s)=[n(s),\xi(s)]^\top$ is the geometric
state, and $\omega(s)$ is the planned-path curvature:
\begin{equation}
\bm z'(s)=\bm f_s(\bm z,\omega;s)=
\begin{bmatrix}
(1-\kappa n)\tan\xi\\[2pt]
(1-\kappa n)\omega/\cos\xi-\kappa
\end{bmatrix}.
\label{eq:spatial_model}
\end{equation}

\section{Game-Supervised Tactical Feasible-Region Framework for Interactive Overtaking}\label{sec:tactical}
% =====================================================================

The proposed tactical layer represents interactive overtaking decisions as
persistent feasible regions for constrained motion planning. A robust
Stackelberg response-set teacher evaluates constraint-admissible tactical
candidates against near-best follower responses and generates a
response-set-optimal action prior together with action-conditioned
robust-value labels. A projected-action TQC actor--critic distills this
supervision into passing-side intention, corridor parameters, and speed
demand. A hysteretic state machine preserves the selected passing side, while
a deterministic opponent-footprint carver converts the learned corridor
parameters and evolving interaction geometry into time-varying Frenet
corridors.


\subsection{Robust Stackelberg Response-Set Supervision}
\label{subsec:game_formulation}
At each tactical update, the encoder combines the two vehicle states, local
track preview, current mode, side lock, and execution health into
$\widetilde{\bm o}_t$. The actor produces
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\bm a^{\rm raw}=\bigl[&L^{\rm cap},R^{\rm cap},d_{\rm safe},
V_{\rm cap}^{\rm req},g_{\rm chase},\\[-1mm]
&g_{\rm ot},g_{\rm abort},b_{\rm lat},w_{\rm safe},
q_{\rm side}\bigr]^\top .
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:action_vec}
\end{equation}
where the components specify the left and right lateral caps, footprint
clearance, requested speed cap, three mode-transition distances, defensive
lateral offset and width, and passing-side intention. Together, they
parameterize a planner-compatible tactical feasible region with speed
guidance. The Euclidean projection $\Pi_{\mathcal A}$ maps the raw action into
the admissible parameter domain by enforcing the configured component bounds,
the ordered transition thresholds
$g_{\rm ot}+\Delta_g\le g_{\rm chase}\le
g_{\rm abort}-\Delta_g$, and the minimum corridor width. This produces the
bound-projected action $\bm a^{\rm B}$, which is subsequently processed by the
tire-admissibility projection $\Pi_{\rm tire}$ to obtain the final tactical
action $\bm a^{\rm F}$.


The offline teacher forms deterministic leader and follower banks by combining
the three side codes with a Sobol design over the remaining action components.
Every candidate is passed through the same bound projection, corridor carver,
and tire screen used online; duplicate or infeasible candidates are discarded.
Let
\(
\bm s_t^G=(\widetilde{\bm o}_t,\bm\chi_t^G)
\)
denote the full rollout state available to the offline teacher, where
\(\bm\chi_t^G\) collects the internal publisher, executor, and one-step
reward memory required to evaluate a coupled candidate preview. The deployed
policy receives only the 51-dimensional observation
\(\widetilde{\bm o}_t\); the additional rollout variables
\(\bm\chi_t^G\) are therefore treated as latent variables when the
full-state teacher labels are transferred to the observation-conditioned
policy. Let \(\mathcal A_i^G(\bm s_t^G)\) denote the candidate bank for
role \(i\).
Each candidate pair induces the coupled executable preview
\begin{equation}
(\mathcal R_e,\mathcal R_o)=
\mathscr P_{H_G}\!\left(
\bm s_t^G;\bm a_e^{\rm F},\bm a_o^{\rm F}\right),
\label{eq:coupled_game_preview}
\end{equation}
where $H_G$ denotes the number of tactical updates in the coupled offline
teacher rollout. The operator $\mathscr P_{H_G}$ recursively applies the stateful
publisher in Algorithm~\ref{alg:tactical_corridor} and the common execution stack.
The candidate pair remains fixed over the preview while both vehicle and
hybrid states evolve jointly; both utilities use the corresponding
role-relative features of this preview.

Let
\(
Z_g=\sum_{k=0}^{H_G-1}\gamma_g^k
\)
and
\(
c_w=\sum_{j=1}^{10}[-w_j]_+
\).
The normalized role-relative utility is
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
U_i(\bm s_t^G,\bm a_e^{\rm F},\bm a_o^{\rm F})
&=\frac{1}{Z_g\|\bm w\|_1}
\sum_{k=0}^{H_G-1}\gamma_g^k\\[-1mm]
&\quad{}\left[\bm w^\top\bm\phi_{i,k}(\mathcal R_e,\mathcal R_o)+c_w\right],
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:game_utility}
\end{equation}
where $\bm\phi_i\in[0,1]^{10}$ contains the role-relative reward features in
\ref{app:parameters}. Hence $U_i\in[0,1]$, and
$\varepsilon_{\rm BR}$ directly specifies the admissible follower-utility gap.
For a fixed ego candidate, let $U_o^{\max}(\bm s_t^G,\bm a_e^{\rm F})$
denote the largest follower utility over $\mathcal A_o^G(\bm s_t^G)$. The
near-best response set is
\begin{subequations}
\label{eq:response_set_teacher}
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\mathfrak R_o^\varepsilon(\bm s_t^G,\bm a_e^{\rm F})
=\bigl\{\bm a_o^{\rm F}\in\mathcal A_o^G(\bm s_t^G):\;&
U_o(\bm s_t^G,\bm a_e^{\rm F},\bm a_o^{\rm F})\\[-1mm]
&\ge U_o^{\max}(\bm s_t^G,\bm a_e^{\rm F})
-\varepsilon_{\rm BR}\bigr\}.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:near_best_response_set}
\end{equation}
The teacher evaluates each candidate against its least favorable retained
response and selects the strongest robust action:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal V_g(\bm s_t^G,\bm a_e^{\rm F})
&=\min_{\bm a_o^{\rm F}\in
\mathfrak R_o^\varepsilon(\bm s_t^G,\bm a_e^{\rm F})}
U_e(\bm s_t^G,\bm a_e^{\rm F},\bm a_o^{\rm F}),\\
\bm a_{\rm th}^{\star}(\bm s_t^G)
&\in\argmax_{\bm a_e^{\rm F}\in\mathcal A_e^G(\bm s_t^G)}
\mathcal V_g(\bm s_t^G,\bm a_e^{\rm F}).
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:robust_teacher_action}
\end{equation}
\end{subequations}
Here $\varepsilon_{\rm BR}=0.05$. Every evaluated ego candidate contributes
the matched sample
$(\widetilde{\bm o}_n,\bm a_{g,n}^{\rm F},v_{g,n},
\bm a_{{\rm th},n}^{\star})$ to $\mathcal D_g$, where
$v_{g,n}=\mathcal V_g(\bm s_n^G,\bm a_{g,n}^{\rm F})$.

Before publication, the requested speed cap is screened against the
speed-dependent tire envelope on the provisional corridor midpoint. For each
trial $v\in\mathcal V_s(V_{\rm cap}^{\rm req})$, define the envelope-unclipped
kinetic-energy template
\begin{equation}
\lambda_k=
\frac{\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\Delta\ell_j}
{\sum_{j=0}^{N_{\rm sta}-2}\Delta\ell_j},
\qquad
\widehat V_k(v)=
\sqrt{V_e^2+\lambda_k\bigl(v^2-V_e^2\bigr)}.
\label{eq:tire_speed_template}
\end{equation}
Using the midpoint curvature $\widehat\kappa_k$, the induced acceleration
demand and peak envelope utilization are
\begin{subequations}
\label{eq:tire_safe_action}
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\widehat a_{T,k}(v)
&=\frac{\widehat V_{k+1}^2(v)-\widehat V_k^2(v)}{2\Delta\ell_k}
+\frac{F_{\rm drag}(\widehat V_k(v))}{m},\\
\widehat a_{N,k}(v)
&=\widehat\kappa_k\widehat V_k^2(v),\\
\widehat\rho(v)
&=\max_{0\le k\le N_{\rm sta}-2}\Phi_{\rm tire}\!\left(
\widehat a_{T,k}(v),\widehat a_{N,k}(v),\widehat V_k(v)\right).
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:tire_preview_demand}
\end{equation}
The projection retains the largest trial cap within the envelope:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
V_{\rm cap}^{\rm F}(\bm a)
&=\max\bigl\{v\in\mathcal V_s(V_{\rm cap}^{\rm req}):
\widehat\rho(v)\le1\bigr\},\\
\Pi_{\rm tire}(\bm a;\widetilde{\bm o})
&=\bm a[V_{\rm cap}^{\rm F}(\bm a)].
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:tire_cap_projection}
\end{equation}
\end{subequations}
Here $V_e$ is the current ego speed, $\mathcal V_s$ is the uniformly sampled
finite grid from $V_{\min}$ to $V_{\rm cap}^{\rm req}$, and $\bm a[v]$ changes
only the cap component. If the grid contains no admissible value,
$\Pi_{\rm tire}$ returns the current-state \textsc{Hold}/braking action
specified in Algorithm~\ref{alg:tactical_corridor}.

\subsection{Game-Supervised Projected-Action TQC}
\label{subsec:theory_guided_rl}
The real-time policy maps the evolving duel geometry to the tactical feasible
set at a fixed computational cost. Its 51-dimensional observation concatenates 27 duel-state features with two
12-dimensional track summaries centered at the ego and opponent. The duel
features contain normalized Frenet states and accelerations, relative progress,
lateral gap and closing rate, time to contact, closing-speed code, side lock, and a
nine-state race-mode code. Each track summary reports mean curvature, peak
absolute curvature, and minimum width over four consecutive bins.

The environment reward is
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
r_t&=\bm w^\top\bm\phi_t,\\
\bm\phi_t&=[\phi_{\rm ot},\phi_{\rm ovb},\phi_{\rm col},\phi_{\rm lat},
\phi_{\rm crush},\phi_{\rm app},\\[-1mm]
&\hspace{9mm}\phi_{\rm sm},\phi_{\rm gs},\phi_{\rm sbs},
\phi_{\rm wide}]^\top .
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:reward_sum}
\end{equation}
\ref{app:parameters} gives the normalized definitions and weights.
The same role-swapped features define Eq.~\eqref{eq:game_utility}, aligning the
teacher and online reward at the level of overtaking progress, clearance,
smoothness, and usable corridor width.

\emph{Projected-action TQC.} Truncated Quantile Critics
(TQC)~\cite{kuznetsov2020tqc} discard the largest
target atoms before the quantile-Huber backup. Environment replay $\mathcal D$
stores the final tactical action $\bm a_t^{\rm F}$. For a raw target-policy
sample, the projected action and truncated target are
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\bm a_{t+1}^{\rm F}&=\Pi_{\rm tire}\!\left(
\Pi_{\mathcal A}(\bm a_{t+1}^{\rm raw});
\widetilde{\bm o}_{t+1}\right),\\
\ell_{t+1}&=\log\pi_\theta(\bm a_{t+1}^{\rm raw}
\mid\widetilde{\bm o}_{t+1}),\\
y_{t,k}&=r_t+\gamma(1-d_t)
\left[z_{(k)}'(\widetilde{\bm o}_{t+1},\bm a_{t+1}^{\rm F})
-\alpha\ell_{t+1}\right],\\
\mathcal Y_t&=\operatorname{sg}\!\left(\{y_{t,k}\}_{k=1}^{K}\right),
\qquad K<2N_q .
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:tqc_truncated_target}
\end{equation}
Thus target critics and replay share the same projected-action support, while
entropy remains the density of the raw stochastic policy. Actor and prior
updates pass gradients through the non-smooth tire search using
\begin{equation}
\bm a^{\rm F,ST}=\bm a^{\rm B}
+\operatorname{sg}\!\left[\bm a^{\rm F}-\bm a^{\rm B}\right].
\label{eq:tire_projection_ste}
\end{equation}
This projected-action surrogate uses the exact projected action forward and an
identity backward map through $\Pi_{\rm tire}$.

Two quantile heads share $\bm h_\zeta=\mathcal E_\zeta^Q(
\widetilde{\bm o},\bm a^{\rm F})$. For matched
$(\widetilde{\bm o}_g,\bm a_g^{\rm F},v_g,
\bm a_{\rm th}^{\star})\sim\mathcal D_g$, let
$\bm h_{\zeta,g}=\mathcal E_\zeta^Q(
\widetilde{\bm o}_g,\bm a_g^{\rm F})$; the updates are
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal L_{\rm critic}&=J_Q+\lambda_g\,
\mathbb E_{\mathcal D_g}\!\left[
\left(g_\psi(\bm h_{\zeta,g})-\mathsf N_V(v_g)\right)^2\right],\\
\mathcal L_{\rm actor}&=J_\pi+\lambda_p\,
\mathbb E_{\mathcal D_g}\!\left[
\left\|\mathsf N_a(\bm\mu_\theta^{\rm F})-
\mathsf N_a(\bm a_{\rm th}^{\star})\right\|_2^2\right].
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:total_loss}
\end{equation}
The offline teacher uses $\bm\chi^G$ as privileged rollout information for
coupled preview evaluation, whereas the deployed actor and critic are
conditioned on $\widetilde{\bm o}$ and
$(\widetilde{\bm o},\bm a^{\rm F})$, respectively. The matched samples in
$\mathcal D_g$ therefore distill the teacher action and response-set value
into observation-conditioned supervision over the rollout contexts represented
in the dataset.

Here $J_Q$ and $J_\pi$ are the standard TQC critic and entropy-regularized
actor losses; $\mathsf N_a$ and $\mathsf N_V$ normalize actions and teacher
values. The actor encoder is independent, whereas the two quantile heads and
the training-only robust-value head share the critic encoder. Consequently,
$\mathcal D$ supplies Bellman transitions, whereas $\mathcal D_g$ supplies
matched teacher-action and response-set-value supervision. The action prior
guides corridor placement, while the auxiliary value labels encourage the
shared critic representation to retain response-set robustness information.

\subsection{Persistent Tactical Feasible-Region Interface}
\label{subsec:corridor_synthesis}
The state machine carries the selected passing opportunity across successive
tactical updates. This temporal persistence complements the spatial corridor:
the policy may refine its width and speed request as the encounter evolves,
while the committed side remains consistent through the pass.
Let $\Delta s_t$ and $\Delta v_t$ be the sampled relative quantities defined in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:rel}.
A feasible side is committed as the ego closes on the opponent, retained
through \textsc{Shadow} and \textsc{Overtake}, and released only after a
confirmed pass, an abort, or an execution-health failure. Health and abort
take precedence over passing, completed passes take precedence over continued
overtaking, and an unlocked ego enters \textsc{Defend} only when a rear
opponent is closing. 
The pass-confirmation indicator $P_t$ debounces the Pass condition defined
after Eq.~\eqref{eq:rel} over $N_{\rm post}$ consecutive tactical updates
before the state machine releases the side lock.

The geometric operation that realizes the selected side is the corridor
carver. For $\mathcal B_{{\rm cap},k}=[\ell_k,u_k]$, the vehicle footprint and
one-sided exclusion are
\begingroup
\setlength{\arraycolsep}{2pt}
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\rho_{{\rm exc},k}&=d_{\rm safe}+\frac12\sum_{j\in\{e,o\}}
(L_j|\sin\xi_{j,k}|+W_j|\cos\xi_{j,k}|),\\
\mathcal B_{{\rm cap},k}&=
[\max\{n_{{\rm trk},k}^{-},-R^{\rm cap}\},
\min\{n_{{\rm trk},k}^{+},L^{\rm cap}\}],\\
\mathcal C_k^{\rm side}(\sigma,\eta)&=\begin{cases}
[\ell_k+\eta[n_{o,k}+\rho_{{\rm exc},k}-\ell_k]_+,u_k],&\sigma=+1,\\
[\ell_k,u_k-\eta[u_k-n_{o,k}+\rho_{{\rm exc},k}]_+],&\sigma=-1.
\end{cases}
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:core_overtake_carver}
\end{equation}
\endgroup
Here $\sigma=+1$ and $-1$ select the left and right passing sides,
respectively. With predicted longitudinal gap $\widehat{\Delta s}_k$,
\textsc{Shadow} uses
$\eta=\operatorname{clip}((g_{\rm chase}-\widehat{\Delta s}_k)/
(g_{\rm chase}-g_{\rm ot}),0,1)$, whereas \textsc{Overtake} uses full
exclusion ($\eta=1$) on the locked side. \textsc{Raceline} uses the capped
track interval; \textsc{Defend} intersects it with a width-$w_{\rm safe}$
interval centered at the clipped offset $n_{o,k}+b_{\rm lat}$; and
\textsc{Hold} retains only the connected component containing the ego vehicle.
Figure~\ref{fig:tactical_mode_semantics} illustrates these geometric semantics.

Algorithm~\ref{alg:tactical_corridor} summarizes the complete route from
offline interaction supervision to online corridor publication. Immediate
contraction and smoothed relaxation allow the feasible region to respond
quickly to reduced space while expanding progressively as the passing channel
opens. The preceding corridor is revalidated for one update when a new
interval loses the required width.

\begin{algorithm}[!tbp]
\caption{Game-supervised tactical-corridor policy}
\label{alg:tactical_corridor}
\footnotesize
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\REQUIRE teacher states and replay $\mathcal D$; online states, track preview,
previous $(m,\sigma^{\rm lock},\mathcal C)$, and execution health
\STATE \textbf{Offline teacher and training:}
\FOR{each teacher state}
\STATE combine Sobol samples with left/neutral/right codes; apply
$\bm a^{\rm raw}\!\rightarrow\!\bm a^{\rm B}\!\rightarrow\!\bm a^{\rm F}$
\STATE discard duplicate or infeasible candidates; generate the coupled
preview~\eqref{eq:coupled_game_preview} for each candidate pair
\STATE evaluate~\eqref{eq:game_utility}, retain near-best follower responses,
and compute~\eqref{eq:response_set_teacher}
\STATE store every matched
$(\widetilde{\bm o},\bm a^{\rm F},v_g,\bm a_{\rm th}^{\star})$
in $\mathcal D_g$
\ENDFOR
\STATE update TQC from $\mathcal D$ and the prior/value terms from $\mathcal D_g$
using Eq.~\eqref{eq:total_loss}
\STATE \textbf{Online tactical publication:}
\STATE sample $\bm a^{\rm raw}$, apply $\Pi_{\mathcal A}$, predict the opponent
at constant Frenet velocity, and evaluate the available side widths
\IF{$h_t$ is invalid, or a locked state satisfies $\Delta s_t\ge g_{\rm abort}$ or
loses $L_{\min}^{\rm corr}$ width}
\STATE set \textsc{Hold} and release the side lock
\ELSIF{the locked ego satisfies the Pass condition defined after
Eq.~\eqref{eq:rel} for $N_{\rm post}$ consecutive updates}
\STATE set $P_t=1$, return to \textsc{Raceline}, and release the lock
\ELSIF{a lock exists, or a non-neutral feasible side is requested with
$0<\Delta s_t\le g_{\rm chase}$ and $\Delta v_t\ge0$}
\STATE retain/commit that side; select \textsc{Shadow} for $\Delta s_t>g_{\rm ot}$
and \textsc{Overtake} otherwise
\ELSIF{$\sigma^{\rm lock}=0$, $-g_{\rm chase}\le \Delta s_t<0$, and
$\Delta v_t<0$}
\STATE select \textsc{Defend}
\ELSE
\STATE select \textsc{Raceline}
\ENDIF
\STATE construct the mode interval using~\eqref{eq:core_overtake_carver} and
the corresponding raceline, defend, or connected-hold rule; use
$\eta=1$ in \textsc{Overtake} and the gap-based ramp in \textsc{Shadow}
\STATE evaluate~\eqref{eq:tire_safe_action}; if no trial cap is admissible,
invoke current-state \textsc{Hold} and braking fallback
\STATE apply immediate contraction and smoothed relaxation; on width failure,
revalidate the preceding corridor for at most one update
\RETURN $(m,\sigma^{\rm lock},V_{\rm cap}^{\rm F},\mathcal C)$ if valid;
otherwise return braking-fallback status
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

Together, the robust Stackelberg response-set teacher, projected-action TQC
policy, and persistent tactical feasible-region interface transform
opponent-aware interaction reasoning into side-consistent Frenet bounds and
speed guidance for constrained motion planning. By adapting the corridor
geometry while preserving the selected passing side, the framework maintains
a consistent overtaking intention throughout the interaction.

\section{Tactical Corridor Execution with Sequential SOCP and \mbox{RC-NMPC}}
\label{sec:execution}\label{sec:planner}\label{sec:controller}
% =====================================================================

The execution layer realizes the persistent tactical feasible region as a
continuous path--speed reference and actuator commands. The published corridor
defines the spatial optimization domain, while the speed request sets the
tactical pace throughout the maneuver.

For the $N=N_{\rm sta}$ spatial stations, let
$\bm z_k=[n_k,\xi_k]^\top$ and $\bm z_T=[n_T,0]^\top$, where $n_T$ is the
clipped raceline in \textsc{Raceline} and the terminal corridor midpoint in
the other modes. The set
$\mathcal Z_k=\{[n,\xi]^\top:\underline n_k\le n\le\overline n_k,
1-\kappa(s_k)n\ge\varepsilon_s,|\xi|\le\xi_{\max}\}$
combines the footprint-eroded corridor and Frenet-domain bounds. Sequential
iteration $j$ linearizes Eq.~\eqref{eq:spatial_model} as
$\widehat{\bm f}_{s,k}^{j}=\bm A_k^j\bm z_k+\bm B_k^j\omega_k+\bm b_k^j$
and solves
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\bm y^{j+1}=\argmin_{\bm y}\;&
\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}w_\omega\sigma_k^\omega
+\bm w_T^\top\bm\epsilon_T\\
\mathrm{s.t.}\;&\bm z_0=[n_e,\xi_e]^\top,\\
&\bm z_{k+1}-\bm z_k=\frac{\Delta s_{\rm sta}}{2}
 (\widehat{\bm f}_{s,k}^{j}+\widehat{\bm f}_{s,k+1}^{j}),\\
&\bm z_k\in\mathcal Z_k,\qquad |\omega_k|\le\omega_{\max},\\
&\left\|[2\omega_k,\sigma_k^\omega-1]^\top\right\|_2
\le\sigma_k^\omega+1,\\
&|\bm z_{N-1}-\bm z_T|\preceq\bm\epsilon_T,\qquad
\bm\epsilon_T\succeq\bm0 .
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:execution_socp}
\end{equation}
Here $\bm y=\{\bm z_k,\omega_k,\sigma_k^\omega,\bm\epsilon_T\}$,
$\bm\epsilon_T=[\epsilon_n,\epsilon_\xi]^\top$,
$\bm w_T=[w_{n,T},w_{\xi,T}]^\top$, and
$\omega_{\max}=\tan\delta_{\max}/(l_f+l_r)$. The dynamics apply for
$k=0,\ldots,N-2$ and the node constraints for $k=0,\ldots,N-1$. The conic
epigraph penalizes squared path curvature, while $\bm\epsilon_T$ softens the
terminal target. Each sequential update is re-evaluated with the nonlinear
Frenet model.

The forward--backward sweep converts the tactical speed request into a
tire-envelope-screened speed reference over the optimized path. Let
$\Delta\ell_k=\|\bm p_{k+1}-\bm p_k\|_2$ and $\kappa_{p,k}$ denote the segment
length and path curvature. The envelope supplies the traction and
braking limits \(a_{T,k}^{+}\) and \(b_{T,k}^{-}\) at
\((\kappa_{p,k}V^2,V)\), after subtracting the resistance acceleration in
traction and adding it in braking.
The steady tactical request is first converted into a braking-reachable
stationwise cap $V_{{\rm cap},k}^{\rm P}$. Intersecting it with the largest
envelope-admissible curvature speed gives
$V_{{\rm lim},k}=\min\{\overline V_{{\rm curv},k},
V_{{\rm cap},k}^{\rm P}\}$. The forward pass starts from $V_0^f=V_e$;
its terminal value initializes
$V_{N-1}^r=\min\{V_{N-1}^{f},V_{{\rm lim},N-1}\}$. The two recursions are
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
V_{k+1}^{f}&=\operatorname{clip}_{[V_{\min},V_{{\rm lim},k+1}]}
\sqrt{(V_k^f)^2+2\Delta\ell_k a_{T,k}^{+}(V_k^f)},\\
V_k^r&=\min\left\{V_k^f,V_{{\rm lim},k},
\sqrt{(V_{k+1}^r)^2+
2\Delta\ell_k b_{T,k+1}^{-}(V_{k+1}^r)}\right\}.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:execution_speed_sweep}
\end{equation}
Evaluated for $k=0,\ldots,N-2$ and $k=N-2,\ldots,0$, the forward and backward
recursions impose acceleration and downstream braking reachability,
respectively. Trapezoidal integration of $1/V_k^r$ provides the time stamps
used to resample the path--speed reference on the controller grid.

The RC-NMPC tracks the resampled path--speed reference on a fixed prediction
grid in an ego-centered local frame. Its state and input are
$\bm\chi_k=[x_k,y_k,\theta_k]^\top$ and
$\bm\nu_k=[V_k,\delta_k]^\top$, respectively. The discrete kinematic bicycle
model is
\begin{subequations}
\label{eq:execution_mpcc}
\begin{equation}
f_{\rm kb}(\bm\chi_k,\bm\nu_k)=\bm\chi_k+T_c
\begin{bmatrix}
V_k\cos\theta_k\\ V_k\sin\theta_k\\
V_k\tan\delta_k/(l_f+l_r)
\end{bmatrix}.
\label{eq:execution_mpcc_model}
\end{equation}
Here $\bm\nu_k^r=[V_k^r,\delta_k^r]^\top$, with
$\delta_k^r=\arctan((l_f+l_r)\kappa_{p,k}^r)$. The quantities
$e_{c,k}=(\bm n_k^r)^\top(\bm p_k-\bm p_k^r)$,
$e_{\psi,k}=\operatorname{wrap}(\theta_k-\theta_k^r)$ define
$\bm e_k=[e_{c,k},e_{\psi,k}]^\top$, and
$\bm Q=\operatorname{diag}(q_c,q_\psi)$. The set
$\mathcal U_{t,k}(\bm\nu_{k-1})$ collects
$|\delta_k|\le\delta_{\max}$,
$|V_k-V_{k-1}|\le\Delta V_{\max}$, and
$|\delta_k-\delta_{k-1}|\le\dot\delta_{\max}T_c$. Its speed component obeys
$V_{\min}\le V_k\le\min\{v_{x,\max},V_{{\rm cap},t,k}^{\rm C}\}$, where the
stagewise cap is obtained by rate-limiting the tactical request from the
previous speed command. Using $\|\bm x\|_{\bm M}^2=\bm x^\top\bm M\bm x$,
the controller solves
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\min_{\bm\nu_{0:N_c-1}}\quad&
q_{\Delta\delta}(\delta_0-\delta_{-1})^2
+\sum_{k=0}^{N_c-1}\left(
\|\bm e_{k+1}\|_{\bm Q}^2
+\|\bm\nu_k-\bm\nu_k^r\|_{\bm R_\nu}^2\right)\\
\mathrm{s.t.}\quad&\bm\chi_0=\bm0,\qquad
\bm\chi_{k+1}=f_{\rm kb}(\bm\chi_k,\bm\nu_k),\\
&\bm\nu_k\in\mathcal U_{t,k}(\bm\nu_{k-1}),
\quad k=0,\ldots,N_c-1.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:execution_mpcc_ocp}
\end{equation}
\end{subequations}
Here $\bm\nu_{-1}=[V_{-1},\delta_{-1}]^\top$ is the command applied in the
preceding control cycle.
% Realized tire demand is reported in Section~\ref{sec:experiments}.

\begin{algorithm}[!tbp]
\caption{Sequential SOCP and RC-NMPC Execution}
\label{alg:executor}
\footnotesize
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\REQUIRE guidance \(\mathcal G_t\), current state, previous valid reference
\STATE erode the track by the ego footprint, intersect it with $\mathcal C_t$,
and choose the mode-dependent terminal target
\STATE initialize the spatial path from the previous valid solution or the
corridor midpoint
\FOR{each configured sequential-convexification iteration}
\STATE solve SOCP~\eqref{eq:execution_socp} and update the linearization
\ENDFOR
\STATE set valid if the path passes the solver and domain checks
\IF{valid}
\STATE query $\mathcal A_{\rm tire}(V)$ and construct
$V_{{\rm cap},k}^{\rm P}$ from $V_{\rm cap}^{\rm F}$, measured speed, and
available braking
\STATE intersect $V_{{\rm cap},k}^{\rm P}$ with the curvature-speed envelope,
run~\eqref{eq:execution_speed_sweep}, and update valid from the tire check
\ENDIF
\IF{valid}
\STATE time-resample the path--speed reference and rate-limit
$V_{{\rm cap},t}^{\rm F}$ to obtain $V_{{\rm cap},t,k}^{\rm C}$
\STATE solve RC-NMPC~\eqref{eq:execution_mpcc} and update valid from its status
\ENDIF
\IF{valid}
\STATE map $(V_{0|t}^{c\star},\delta_{0|t}^{c\star})$ to throttle, brake,
steering, and gear
\ELSE
\STATE revalidate the previous reference for one cycle; otherwise request
current-state \textsc{Hold} and issue rate-limited braking with the preceding
steering angle
\ENDIF
\RETURN four-channel command and health status
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Experimental platform and configuration}
\label{subsec:hil_env}\label{subsec:param_setup}

The Abu Dhabi Autonomous Racing League (A2RL) provides a full-scale head-to-head racing environment for EAV24 vehicles on Yas Marina North, together with the high-fidelity AutoVerse simulation platform. Using this platform, we established a hardware-in-the-loop (HIL) system that coupled the simulator with the Neousys RGS-8805GC onboard computer used on the EAV24 race car in the full-scale competition (Fig.~\ref{fig:a2rl_sim}). Both policy training and the in-domain HIL evaluation use Yas Marina North. The AutoVerse simulator ran on a separate simulation server and generated LiDAR and GNSS/IMU data, which were transmitted to the onboard computer over a dedicated Ethernet link using DDSI--RTPS over UDP/IP. All five software modules executed on the RGS-8805GC, which returned throttle, brake, steering, and gear commands to the simulated vehicle through CAN.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}
% =====================================================================

High-speed overtaking on narrow closed circuits is constrained by limited maneuvering space, increasing the risk of failed passes and collisions. Repeated replanning further complicates the preservation of a consistent passing intention while retaining sufficient geometric freedom for downstream planning and control. To address these challenges, this paper developed a game-guided tactical-corridor decision framework that formulates interactive overtaking as robust tactical feasible-set selection. A robust Stackelberg response-set teacher provides a response-set-optimal action prior and action-conditioned robust-value labels for training a Truncated Quantile Critics (TQC) policy that predicts passing-side intention, corridor parameters, and speed demand. A hysteretic state machine preserves the selected passing side, while deterministic opponent-footprint carving adapts the feasible region to the evolving track and interaction geometry. The resulting side-locked, time-varying Frenet corridor and speed guidance are realized through sequential second-order cone programming (SOCP) planning and reference-conditioned contouring-error nonlinear model predictive control (RC-NMPC).

Monte Carlo closed-loop experiments were conducted on the EAV24 hardware-in-the-loop platform. The proposed framework achieved a 94.7\% overtaking success rate, a mean overtaking time of 4.82~s, and a planning-feasibility rate of 1.000, with no collisions or track-boundary violations. Compared with three matched baselines using the same execution stack, the overtaking success rate was 31.2--66.2 percentage points higher and the mean overtaking time was 3.01--6.41~s shorter. Ablation studies further confirmed the roles of adaptive corridor shaping and persistent side commitment. Using the same policy and execution parameters, the framework maintained overtaking success rates of 91.8\% and 88.9\% on the unseen Imola and Suzuka circuits, respectively. These results demonstrate that persistent tactical feasible regions provide an effective interface between game-guided overtaking decisions and constrained closed-loop execution, highlighting the framework's practical potential for high-speed autonomous racing.

Future work will leverage participation in the Abu Dhabi Autonomous Racing League (A2RL) to deploy the proposed framework on a full-scale autonomous race car and conduct high-speed on-track validation. The framework will also be extended from the current two-vehicle setting to multi-vehicle racing scenarios to evaluate its performance under more complex competitive interactions.
