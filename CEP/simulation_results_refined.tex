\subsection{Target-hardware platform and evaluation protocol}
\label{subsec:hil_env}\label{subsec:param_setup}

The complete tactical, planning, control, and actuator-allocation stack ran on
the Neousys RGS-8805GC industrial computer installed on the A2RL EAV24
platform~\cite{a2rl2024,hoffmann2026a2rl,neousysrgs}. The EAV24 plant and Yas
Marina environment ran on a separate server (Fig.~\ref{fig:a2rl_sim}). Ego,
opponent, and race-control states entered the target computer through two
virtual Controller Area Network (CAN) channels. Throttle percentage, brake
pressure, steering-actuator position, and requested gear returned through the
same vehicle interface. ROS~2 streams provided instrumentation without
replacing this controller-under-test boundary.

\begin{figure}[!tbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{sim_shuoming.pdf}
\caption{Target-hardware HIL topology. The production algorithm executes on
the RGS-8805GC vehicle computer, while the EAV24 plant and track run on a
separate server. Vehicle, opponent, and race-control states cross the dual-CAN
boundary toward the algorithm. The four actuator channels cross it toward the
plant.}
\label{fig:a2rl_sim}
\end{figure}

The configured plant includes a six-speed mapped powertrain and limited-rate
throttle, brake, and steering actuators. It also models suspension, anti-roll,
aerodynamic, tire-relaxation, rolling-resistance, wheel-inertia, thermal, and
collision effects. Ego and opponent states are generated at 100~Hz,
GNSS/attitude at 100~Hz, and IMU data at 250~Hz. The controller acts at 20~Hz.
Camera, lidar, and radar models are configured, but the evaluation uses
simulator-provided vehicle states. This choice isolates tactical decision and
execution from perception. The setup tests target-hardware timing, transport,
solver fallback, actuator mapping, and subsystem-resolved plant response. It
does not replace an instrumented track test~\cite{sagmeister2024fidelity}.

Two representative regimes expose complementary tactical demands. Scenario~I
is a high-speed closure with a short commitment window. Scenario~II is a
prolonged sharp-corner engagement that demands persistent side selection while
both vehicles decelerate and re-accelerate. These runs provide mechanism-level
evidence, whereas the matched 1000-lap campaigns provide aggregate evidence.
The author-confirmed aggregate schedule was fixed across methods, although its
per-lap scenario allocation is unavailable in the current archive. The plant,
track, path and speed planners, MPCC, and actuator allocator remained fixed in
all matched comparisons.

One closed-loop lap is the aggregate analysis unit. Overtaking success rate
(OSR) is the proportion of laps in which longitudinal order changes while
lateral clearance is retained within the evaluation horizon. Time to overtake
(TTO), per-lap minimum gap, collision count, mean time to collision (TTC),
track-boundary violation, and path-feasibility ratio (PFR) provide complementary
effectiveness and execution measures.

The principal OSR is reported with a two-sided 95\% exact binomial
(Clopper--Pearson) interval. This interval treats laps as Bernoulli units and
does not model scenario or training-seed clustering. Five independently
trained policies provide a separate seed-sensitivity summary as mean $\pm$
standard deviation of seed-level OSRs. Pairwise success-count tests in
Tables~\ref{tab:comparison} and~\ref{tab:ablation} use two-sided
two-proportion $z$ tests. Holm correction is applied within each
four-comparison family. These tests are descriptive because the unavailable
per-lap archive prevents hierarchical scenario and seed analysis. The supplied
aggregate verifier reproduces all integer counts, confidence intervals, tests,
and table entries without reconstructing lap-level observations.

\subsection{Closed-loop evidence in two contrasting racing regimes}
\label{subsec:representative_cases}

The two representative runs are deterministic mechanism demonstrations, not
independent replicates. Table~\ref{tab:case_summary} reports their logged
outcomes. Each case follows the same evidence chain. Spatial snapshots first
show the tactical opportunity selected by the game-guided policy. Corridor,
mode, and cap histories then test whether that decision persists under
replanning. Tracking responses test whether the common executor realizes the
published feasible set. Relative separation and tire-force utilization provide
supporting clearance and physical-admissibility diagnostics. This hierarchy
keeps the paper's main claim on interactive corridor selection rather than on
the shared safety and control layers.

\subsubsection*{Scenario I: short-window high-speed closure}

Scenario~I tests whether interaction reasoning reaches the planner before the
passing window closes. The ego speed increased from 68.73 to
73.10~m~s$^{-1}$, while the opponent remained near 57.6~m~s$^{-1}$. The FSM
first entered \textsc{Shadow} at 0.74~s and \textsc{Overtake} at 3.19~s. The
first later negative $\Delta s$ occurred at 4.32~s, giving the 1.13-s interval
in Table~\ref{tab:case_summary}.

Figure~\ref{fig:case02_engagement} makes the game-layer decision spatially
auditable. At 3.1~s, the policy exposes a continuous corridor and a
finite-horizon plan before the ego reaches the opponent. By 4.3~s, the same
side is retained as $\Delta s$ approaches zero. This representation gives the
downstream solver a usable region, not only a discrete maneuver label or an
instantaneous target point. The two snapshots therefore show the principal
advantage of the interface: strategic commitment remains explicit while the
executor retains freedom to optimize the path inside it.

Figure~\ref{fig:case02_execution} connects the tactical decision to closed-loop
motion. The path panel samples the latest non-future corridor at each ego
station. All 108 samples with an available corridor remained inside it, with a
minimum lateral margin of 0.52~m. The speed panel shows sustained closure,
whereas acceleration and heading record the commands needed to realize the
side change. All 109 acceleration samples satisfy the plotted speed-dependent
envelope, with a maximum normalized expression of 0.150. The combined evidence
shows that the learned feasible set survives conversion into a dynamically
realized reference rather than ending as an unexecuted tactical choice.

\subsubsection*{Scenario II: persistent sharp-corner overtake}

Scenario~II removes the simple advantage of sustained high-speed closure. The
ego decelerates from 65.83 to 12.46~m~s$^{-1}$ before accelerating out of the
corner, while the opponent follows the same qualitative speed cycle. The FSM
enters \textsc{Shadow} at 0.38~s and \textsc{Overtake} at 2.59~s. It then
retains \textsc{Overtake} until the run ends. The first negative $\Delta s$
sample occurs at 11.22~s, giving an 8.63-s committed interval.

Figure~\ref{fig:case05_engagement} shows why a persistent feasible set matters
in this regime. The first snapshot reserves a curved region before the apex,
with the opponent 17.8~m ahead. The second preserves a consistent exit-side
region as the longitudinal order changes. A transient target point could move
across the opponent as curvature and relative speed evolve. In contrast, the
published corridor keeps the selected passing opportunity visible across the
entire turn and leaves path regularity to the common executor.

\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{final0205_case_05_engagement_two_panel_v3.pdf}
\caption{Scenario~II interaction geometry. (a) The corridor and ego plan
prepare the sharp-corner pass with $\Delta s=17.8$~m. (b) The same tactical

\label{fig:case05_engagement}

Figure~\ref{fig:case05_execution} shows execution under stronger curvature and
speed variation. All 229 samples with an available non-future corridor remain
inside the published bounds, with a minimum margin of 0.25~m. The path changes
side through the corner while speed falls by more than 50~m~s$^{-1}$ and the
heading error spans the turn. This continuity is the algorithmic advantage:
the game layer reshapes the feasible region while the fixed executor resolves
the coupled path and speed response. The acceleration panel is diagnostic and
uses the declared envelope display mask; it is not evidence that every plant
sample satisfies the reduced envelope.

Figure~\ref{fig:case05_tactical} isolates the two mechanisms that sustain the
pass. The raw FSM record progresses once from \textsc{Raceline} to
\textsc{Shadow} and then to \textsc{Overtake}, without display normalization.
The left and right caps vary from 3.03--5.99~m and 2.14--6.00~m, respectively.
This asymmetric adaptation retains usable width while the track geometry
changes. Despite the extended maneuver, lateral RMS remains 0.051~m, with a
0.147-m maximum absolute error. Speed RMS is 1.82~m~s$^{-1}$, with a
3.50-m~s$^{-1}$ maximum. Thus the adaptive and persistent corridor reaches the
vehicle as a trackable reference rather than repeatedly restarting the planner.

Figure~\ref{fig:case05_safety} separates clearance evidence from the limits of
the reduced physical model. No sample enters both rectangular contact bands;
when $|\Delta s|\le5$~m, the minimum $|\Delta n|$ is 5.26~m. This supports
clearance during the order change. The tire panel shows only the 193 samples
that satisfy $\rho\le1$ under the fitted envelope. The remaining 37 of 230
logged samples are excluded by the plotting rule, and the maximum raw
expression is 2.284. Consequently, the panel describes the in-envelope
operating distribution but cannot certify universal closed-loop envelope
satisfaction. This discrepancy bounds the physical-admissibility claim without
altering the evidence for persistent tactical-corridor execution.

\subsection{Failure modes and validity boundary}
\label{subsec:failure_modes}

The comparisons expose the pain points targeted by the method.
Non-Interactive MPC retained clearance but missed transient passing windows.
The fixed corridor could not reshape the usable track region, and
non-persistent guidance revised intent faster than the executor could absorb.
These failures reduce OSR and PFR even though the plant, tire-force limits,
planner, and controller are shared. The full method's 53 non-successes were
non-completions within the evaluation horizon. The available aggregate record
does not support a finer causal partition. Zero observed collisions therefore
means zero events in these 1000 laps, not a formal safety guarantee.

The two case records also expose narrower evidence boundaries. One Scenario~I
mode sample is normalized only in the display ribbon. The raw value remains in
the archived log. In Scenario~II, 37 measured acceleration samples exceed the
fitted reduced-order envelope and are omitted by the supplied plotting mask.
This prevents interpreting the tire panel as universal plant-level constraint
satisfaction. It does not change the logged corridor containment, mode
persistence, tracking, or separation results. Future work should reconcile the
preview envelope with measured high-fidelity plant accelerations and should
show every point or a formally justified residual diagnostic.

The present evidence is bounded to two-vehicle, simulator-state-informed HIL.
It does not establish robustness to missed detections, occlusion, multiple
opponents, changing grip, tire degradation, damage, or opponents outside the
training behavior distribution. Moreover, the archived aggregates do not
retain the per-lap scenario and seed map needed for clustered uncertainty or
paired lap-level effect intervals. These are explicit targets for the next
campaign and for controlled EAV24 track validation.
